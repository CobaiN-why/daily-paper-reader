Abstract 

The evolution of linguistic style in conversa-tions is an underexplored issue in NLP. Most style-control datasets focus on sentences or assume a static style throughout, missing the dynamic shifts that occur as user preferences change during interactions. We introduce C ON -

> VO

DRIFT , a dataset designed to model pro-gressive stylistic conversational tone drift un-der fixed semantic intent. It is built on 15,727 shared multi-turn conversational structures for adaptation and persona-conditioned alignment methods. It consists of six prompt–response pairs per conversation, each with the annotation of style drift and style direction labels. These pairs cover a range of communication genres. We further derive a complementary pairwise dataset by pairing semantically equivalent but stylistically distinct responses and annotating persona-conditioned preferences using five dis-tinct style communication personas, enabling the controlled study of personalisation and plu-ralistic alignment in language tone. In addition to dataset construction, we conduct a compre-hensive evaluation involving human validation, LLM-as-judge assessment, and automatic lex-ical and semantic evaluations. Across seven Likert criteria annotated by three human an-notators, the average Krippendorff’s α is 0.88, and our lexical and semantic analyses show that drift events induce lexical changes while preserving semantic similarity. 

Keywords: Pluralistic alignment, Preference learn-ing, Conversational tone drift 

1 Introduction 

Conversational interactions involving language models are inherently dynamic: users often ad-just linguistic style and tone across multiple turns as goals, preferences, and stylistic expectations evolve. However, existing Natural Language Pro-cessing (NLP) datasets and benchmarks provide limited support for modelling this kind of progres-sive, turn-by-turn stylistic change. In particular, most style-control and style-transfer benchmarks operate at the sentence level (Rao and Tetreault, 2018) and explicitly ignore conversational context, making them ill-suited for studying stylistic evolu-tion over multi-turn dialogue. (Roy et al., 2023). This highlights the lack of datasets that capture how tone progressively drifts during a conversation while preserving the context and maintaining the original semantic intent. We introduce C ONVO DRIFT 1, a flexible dataset built around a shared conversational benchmark. This corpus facilitates the study of progressive stylistic tone drift in multi-turn dialogue and the derivation of style and tone-relevant prefer-ence data suitable for personalised alignment via Reinforcement Learning From Human Feedback (RLHF), addressing existing limitations in the field. By explicitly separating semantic content from stylistic modification, C ONVO DRIFT enables con-trolled analysis of tone variation across turns with-out confounding content changes, a property that is difficult to achieve with existing dialogue cor-pora (Cheng et al., 2020). The same conversa-tional trajectories can be reused for multiple pur-poses, including modelling stylistic dynamics or serving as preference supervision, and can be eval-uated under different preference profiles through persona-conditioned tone drift. This design natu-rally supports pluralistic alignment, in which mul-tiple stylistic preferences are treated as valid rather than collapsed into a single reward signal, address-ing concerns highlighted in recent alignment re-search (Poddar et al., 2024). To further demonstrate the practical utility of the dataset, we conducted a downstream preference modelling task using Vari-

2 Related Work 

2.1 Stylistic Tone Variation and Drift in Text 

Research on stylistic control in text has histori-cally focused on style transfer, in which a single input sentence is rewritten to exhibit a target stylis-tic attribute while preserving its semantic content. Foundational datasets such as the Grammarly Ya-hoo Answers Formality Corpus ( GYAFC ) (Rao and Tetreault, 2018) enable supervised informal–formal rewriting and have become standard benchmarks for formality transfer. The existing research has explored stylistic attributes, including polite-ness (Danescu-Niculescu-Mizil et al., 2013; Niu and Bansal, 2018), sentiment (Fu et al., 2018) , pro-fessionalism (Ficler and Goldberg, 2017), and con-trolled generation (Hu et al., 2017) without parallel data (Shen et al., 2017). Despite covering a range of stylistic attributes, these approaches predomi-nantly operate at the sentence level, framing stylis-tic control as a single-turn rewriting task applied to isolated utterances. In these formulations, stylistic transformation is treated as a context-independent operation, where each input is processed without access to conversational history or discourse struc-ture (Ficler and Goldberg, 2017; Hu et al., 2017). As a result, such methods do not capture stylistic dynamics across a conversation, where tone may evolve gradually in response to prior turns or com-municative goals. More recent work has begun to incorporate di-alogue context into stylistic modelling. Roy et al. (2023) propose a few-shot conversational style transfer framework that rewrites entire dialogues conditioned on exemplar conversations. Neverthe-less, this line of work focuses on global style trans-formation rather than planned, turn-by-turn stylis-tic drift, leaving fine-grained conversational tone evolution underexplored. Although conversational style transfer incorpo-rates dialogue context, it still provides limited su-pervision for when stylistic changes occur and how 

tone evolves progressively across turns. Modelling such tone evolution also introduces a personalisa-tion dimension, as different users may exhibit and prefer different stylistic behaviours throughout an interaction. This observation naturally connects stylistic drift modelling to research on personalised and persona-conditioned dialogue systems, which explicitly account for user-specific conversational behaviours. This connection between stylistic variation and user-specific behaviour motivates consideration of persona-based dialogue research. While such re-search captures user identity and preferences at the level of content and conversational consis-tency it offers limited mechanisms for modelling how stylistic preferences (e.g., tone and writing style) are learned, compared, or optimised during training, despite their central role in personalisa-tion (Zhang et al., 2024). Addressing this limitation requires preference-based learning frameworks that explicitly model human judgements, motivating the growing role of preference data and RLHF. 

2.2 Personalised Dialogues and Alignment 

Personalisation in dialogue systems has tradition-ally been approached through persona condition-ing, where responses are generated based on user-specific attributes or profiles (Zhang et al., 2018). Li et al. (2016) demonstrated that conditioning on textual persona descriptions improves speaker con-sistency and engagement in open-domain dialogue. Subsequent research extended this paradigm by in-corporating demographic and contextual attributes such as age, gender, and emotion into dialogue systems (Zhao et al., 2025b), influencing response content and style. While prior work shows that per-sonas shape stylistic behaviour (Joshi et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2025a; Lee et al., 2025), these ap-proaches typically treat style as a static property of a user or conversation, rather than as a signal that evolves across interaction turns. At the same time, surveys on personalised lan-guage models highlight tone and writing style as key personalisation factors, alongside relevance and accuracy (Zhang et al., 2024). Effective per-sonalisation, therefore, requires models to adapt to users’ stylistic preferences across different con-texts, rather than producing an average response. However, most existing preference datasets do not explicitly label style or capture how stylistic align-ment changes over multi-turn conversations. This limitation motivates the development of conversa-tional datasets that jointly model style, persona, and preference dynamics over time. RLHF has become the dominant paradigm for aligning language models with human preferences, typically using pairwise comparisons aggregated into a single reward signal (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022; Ziegler et al., 2019). While recent work explores personalisation and pluralistic alignment by modelling heterogeneous or user-conditioned preferences (Poddar et al., 2024; Park et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2023), existing RLHF datasets primar-ily focus on global qualities such as helpfulness, safety, and correctness. Stylistic factors, particu-larly tone and writing style, are rarely annotated ex-plicitly, and preference signals are generally treated as static rather than context-dependent. Taken together, prior work on personalised di-alogue and preference-based alignment provides mechanisms for modelling who the speaker is and 

what they prefer, but offers limited support for cap-turing how stylistic expression evolves over the course of a conversation. Most datasets either ab-stract away conversational context or collapse pref-erences into a single reward signal, limiting their ability to capture fine-grained, progressive stylistic changes. These limitations highlight shortcomings in existing resources for studying dynamic tone adaptation and stylistic alignment in multi-turn con-versations. 

3 Methodology 

CONVO DRIFT is a multi-stage data generation and validation dataset designed to support both the analysis of multi-turn stylistic tone drift and persona-conditioned preference learning for per-sonalised RLHF. Accordingly, the dataset con-sists of two core stages, each producing a distinct but tightly coupled dataset. (1) C ONVO DRIFT –Conversation: A multi-turn conversational dataset that captures progressive stylistic tone drift under fixed semantic intent. (2) C ONVO DRIFT –RLHF: A persona-conditioned pairwise preference dataset constructed from conversational trajectories to en-able personalised and pluralistic alignment. As shown in Figure 1, both datasets reuse the same multi-turn prompts and responses. The RLHF subset restructures responses from these conversa-tions into style-contrasting preference pairs. This unified design ensures consistency between conver-sational structure and preference supervision, en-abling C ONVO DRIFT to serve as both a diagnostic benchmark for tone drift and a resource for study-ing personalised and pluralistic alignment (Poddar et al., 2024). To provide empirical evidence of the dataset’s effectiveness, we conducted a down-stream preference modelling task using VPL with representations of GPT-2 and Llama 3.1 8B, achiev-ing accuracies of 0.579 and 0.65, respectively, with further details provided in Appendix A. 

3.1 Conversational Dataset Creation 

This section describes how C ONVO DRIFT was con-structed to capture progressive conversational tone changes while preserving semantic intent across multiple turns. 

3.1.1 Model Selection and Generation Setup 

To select appropriate models for data generation, we conducted a preliminary comparison across 12 Large Language Models (LLMs) using an identical set of conversational prompts. Models were evalu-ated based on output quality, adherence to stylistic instructions, latency, and estimated token-level cost under a fixed input–output budget. Based on this comparison, we selected GPT-4.1-mini as the primary model for conversational response genera-tion due to its consistent output quality and reliable stylistic control, and GPT-5-nano for generating explicit drift, direction labels due to its higher qual-ity and lower latency outputs. Using this setup, we generated 15,727 multi-turn conversations, each containing six prompt–response pairs, resulting in a total of 94,362 response turns. This has been discussed in detail in Appendix B.

CONVO DRIFT generates conversations across mul-tiple genres as shown in Table 1 with distinct stylis-tic norms, spanning both professional and informal contexts. Each genre is paired with diverse com-municative scenarios, and prompts enforce seman-tic preservation while allowing controlled stylistic variation. Generated conversations are then filtered using a rule-based quality pipeline to ensure struc-tural consistency, including a fixed conversation length, valid drift annotations, and the presence of multiple stylistic drift events. 3.2 Conversion to RLHF Pairwise Data 

This section explains how conversational trajecto-ries are transformed into semantically aligned but stylistically contrasting response pairs for persona-conditioned preference learning. 

3.2.1 Pairwise Preference Construction 

Each multi-turn conversation in C ONVO DRIFT con-sists of a sequence of prompts that progressively request stylistic refinements to a response, produc-ing a trajectory of responses with shared semantic intent but differing stylistic realisations. To derive preference supervision suitable for RLHF, we construct pairwise preference instances by selecting a prompt p from the conversation and pairing two responses associated with it. Specifi-cally, the initial response generated for the task is treated as one candidate ( ya), while a later response from the same conversation corresponding to an explicit stylistic drift with a known direction (e.g., casual or formal) is selected as the contrasting can-didate ( yb). 3.2.2 Persona-based Preference Learning 

CONVO DRIFT supports persona-conditioned pref-erence learning by leveraging the explicit stylis-tic annotations available in the underlying con-versational dataset. Since each response is la-belled with drift and direction tags (e.g., toward casual/expressive or formal/precise), stylistic pref-erences can be systematically grouped and evalu-ated without relying on implicit or noisy preference inference. As an experimental instantiation, we define five communication personas, each encoding a consis-tent stylistic bias shown in Table 2. These personas are not intended to model user identity, but rather to represent distinct and interpretable evaluation perspectives over stylistic variation. For each example, which includes a prompt and two responses, each persona independently chooses the response that best matches its communication style. This produces separate preference labels for each persona. By keeping these persona-specific labels instead of combining them into a single de-cision, the dataset preserves differences in stylistic preferences. This design allows us to study person-alised and pluralistic alignment, in which multiple communication styles are treated as valid at the same time (Cheng et al., 2023; Poddar et al., 2024). 4 Evaluation and Experiments 

4.1 Conversational Dataset 

This section evaluates the reliability and quality of the conversational dataset through human valida-tion, LLM-as-a-judge assessment, and automatic semantic and lexical analyses. 

4.1.1 Human Evaluation 

We conducted a human evaluation to assess the correctness of tone drift annotations and the over-all quality of multi-turn conversations in C ONVO -DRIFT . Given the cost and time of manual vali-dation, three independent annotators evaluated a stratified subset of the dataset using a sampling strategy designed to balance coverage with reliable agreement estimation. Since human validation is particularly valuable but limited, we used a controlled-overlap design to maximise the number of human-validated con-versations while still enabling agreement analysis: 175 conversations were annotated by all annotators (gold set), 300 conversations were annotated by two annotators (100 conversations per annotator pair), and 375 conversations were annotated by a single annotator (125 unique conversations per an-notator). This results in 1,500 conversation-level ratings in total (500 per annotator), covering 850 unique conversations. The communication genre balance was maintained across all subsets, ensuring each genre was represented in similar proportions. Inter-annotator agreement metrics were computed exclusively on the gold set, while the remaining an-notated data was used for qualitative analysis and downstream evaluation. Annotators evaluated each conversation on seven ordinal criteria using a five-point Likert scale. We measure agreement on these ordinal ratings using Krippendorff’s α for ordinal data, which supports multiple annotators (Krippendorff, 2011), and re-port the resulting scores in Table 3. In addition to the scalar ratings, annotators were instructed to correct drift flags and tone direction labels whenever inconsistencies were identified. Drift is annotated as a binary value indicating whether a tone change is requested in the prompt, while direction labels specify the direction of tone change (no change, formal to casual, or casual to formal). 4.1.2 LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation 

In addition to human validation, we employed an LLM-as-a-judge framework (Zheng et al., 2023). After human evaluation, the remaining portion of the dataset was evaluated using Gemini 3Flash as an automatic judge. The LLM was prompted to assign drift labels, direction labels, and conversation-level quality ratings using the same annotation guidelines followed by human annota-tors. To assess the reliability of the LLM judge, its outputs were compared against a gold-standard human reference constructed via majority voting over human annotations. Agreement for the drift and direction labels was measured using Cohen’s 

κ. The LLM demonstrates strong agreement with human validation for tone drift detection, achieving Drift of 0.888 , and substantial agreement for tone direction labelling with Direction of 0.930 (Cohen, 1960). These results indicate that the LLM reliably identifies when tone changes occur and correctly captures their directional shift, while aligning with human annotator decisions.

5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we introduced C ONVO DRIFT , a benchmark for modelling progressive stylistic tone drift in multi-turn conversations. By utilising a shared conversational structure, we explicitly sep-arate semantic intent from stylistic variation, en-abling the derivation of both multi-turn drift trajec-tories and persona-conditioned preference datasets for pluralistic alignment. Comprehensive valida-tion via human assessment, LLM-as-a-judge, and lexical metrics confirms that this preserves seman-tic meaning while capturing structured stylistic dy-namics. 

6 Limitations and Future Directions 

There are several avenues remain for future ex-ploration to address the constraints of our current approach. First, as a synthetically generated dataset designed to emulate human–LLM conversations, it lacks the variability and spontaneity inherent in real-world organic data; thus, future work may in-clude real human conversations to capture this re-alism. Second, we currently measure tone varia-tions only between “formal” and “casual” styles, so future research can explore more complex emo-tions and subtle style shifts. Third, all conversa-tions are constrained to a fixed length of six turns, limiting the analysis of longer-range stylistic de-pendencies and naturally varying dialogue lengths. Fourth, as the dataset is predominantly syntheti-cally generated and LLM-annotated, it may inherit model-specific stylistic patterns and annotation bi-ases that do not fully reflect naturally occurring human–LLM interactions. Finally, persona con-ditioning is restricted to five predefined personas; expanding this space to include a broader range of user preferences would further strengthen the dataset’s relevance for personalisation and pluralis-tic alignment research.

A Downstream Validation with VPL 

To examine the effectiveness of the proposed dataset beyond direct label annotation, we con-ducted a downstream validation experiment us-ing Variational Preference Learning (VPL) (Poddar et al., 2024). The purpose of this task was to show the effectiveness of the proposed drift and direc-tion labels in providing useful learning signals for preference modelling, and to assess whether the dataset captures meaningful persona-specific pref-erence patterns. VPL is well-suited for this analy-sis because it represents preference learning as a latent-variable problem, where the latent variable 

z captures hidden user or annotator context and enables the reward model to predict preferences conditioned on that context, rather than averaging all preferences into a single reward function. 

Figure 4: t-SNE visualisation of the latent representa-tions learned by the Llama 3.1 8B-based VPL model. Figure 5: t-SNE visualisation of the latent representa-tions learned by the GPT-2-based VPL model. 

Following this setup, we used the prompt to-gether with response A and response B from our pairwise dataset, treating Persona A and Persona B as two distinct preference contexts. The model was trained to predict the preferred response un-der the corresponding persona-conditioned setting. We evaluated the VPL-based model using two rep-resentation backbones: GPT-2 and Llama 3.1 8B. The GPT-2-based model achieved an accuracy of 0.579, while the model using Llama 3.1 8B repre-sentations achieved a higher accuracy of 0.65. This indicates that the proposed labels provide predic-tive signal for distinguishing persona-dependent preferences. The stronger performance of Llama 3.1 8B is likely due to its larger parameter capacity and richer representation quality, which allow it to capture more fine-grained stylistic and persona-conditioned differences. We further visualised the learned latent represen-tations using t-SNE plots. The resulting visualisa-tions show clearer clustering for the Llama 3.1 8B-based model, with two distinct clusters correspond-ing to Persona A and Persona B. This separation suggests that the model learned structured differ-ences between the two persona-conditioned pref-erence distributions. Therefore, the downstream experiment provides additional evidence that the proposed drift and direction labels are meaningful, and that the dataset supports persona-sensitive pref-erence learning. Since VPL is designed to learn separable latent representations for divergent user preferences, these results strengthen the validity of our dataset for pluralistic and adaptive preference modelling. 

B Prompts used for dataset generation 

The dataset was generated using a structured prompting strategy designed to produce multi-turn conversations exhibiting controlled stylistic tone drift while preserving semantic intent. For each communication domain, a dedicated prompt tem-plate was used to guide the generation process. All prompts enforce a fixed conversation length of six prompt–response pairs and require the assistant to rewrite the full response at every turn based on incremental user feedback. Across all domains, the first prompt in each conversation requests an initial draft and is explic-itly constrained to have no tone drift. Subsequent prompts introduce natural, user-like revision re-quests. Tone drift is annotated at the transition level and is allowed only when a noticeable change in tone occurs, such as formal to casual or vice versa. To ensure realism, each conversation con-tains a minimum of two and a maximum of three tone-drift transitions distributed organically across turns two to six. All prompts enforce strict output constraints, including a fixed JSON schema, full response rewrites at every turn, and prohibition of place-holders. This design ensures consistency across domains while allowing stylistically diverse refine-ments. The following sections provide the exact prompt templates used for each communication genre.
