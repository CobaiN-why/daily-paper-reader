ABSTRACT 

Next-token prediction (NTP) is the standard pretraining objective for large language models, yet it provides an explicit training signal only for the immediate next token, which can lead models to exploit local patterns instead of capturing longer-range structure and ideas. Multi-token prediction (MTP) addresses this by training models to predict several future tokens. However, existing MTP methods often introduce a large number of new parameters with limited improvements in downstream performance. Latent MTP approaches address this efficiency issue by encoding future tokens into a vector representation. However, these approaches usually rely on external helper models for future token encoding. We propose LightMTP, a lightweight, i.e., parameter-efficient, latent MTP approach that bootstraps the future token representations from the model’s own hidden states. Our two LightMTP variants extend supervision to more future tokens without requiring the additional computational overhead of conventional MTP nor the external supervision latent MTP normally relies on. LightMTP adds at most 1% extra parameters, retains better performance on general language modeling benchmarks, and achieves similar gains in planning, coding, and reasoning. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Next-token prediction (NTP) is the standard pretraining objective for large language models. It requires no supervision beyond raw text, computes the loss at every position in a single teacher-forced forward pass, and improves predictably with scale (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022). However, NTP provides an explicit training signal only for the immediate next token. Information about more distant tokens is learned only insofar as it helps predict the next one, which can lead models to exploit local patterns instead of capturing longer-range structure. Bachmann & Nagarajan (2024) show that teacher-forced NTP can fail to learn even simple planning tasks. Multi-token prediction (MTP) addresses this limitation by additionally training the model to predict the tokens xt+2 , . . . , x t+k from its representation at position t. This densifies the training signal and rewards representations that encode information beyond the next token (Gloeckle et al., 2024; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025). Most MTP methods operate at the token level, attaching one prediction module per future offset that outputs a vocabulary distribution. However, this is costly: each offset adds a vocabulary projection, so compute and memory grow with both k and the vocabulary size, and many designs add highly parametrized transformer layers per offset. Additionally, token-level MTP can also interfere with next-token prediction. Gloeckle et al. (2024) find that it hurts models below roughly 3B parameters. Latent MTP methods avoid the vocabulary projection by predicting dense representations of future tokens. (Yin et al., 2024; Mahajan et al., 2026). We propose LightMTP, a lightweight latent MTP objective that bootstraps its targets from the model’s own hidden states (Figure 1). A small MLP head reads the last hidden state at position t and directly predicts the intermediate layer states of future positions up to k steps ahead. Because the targets are latent, the model is encouraged to encode information about upcoming tokens without committing to specific ones. LightMTP requires no auxiliary model, avoids projecting onto the vocabulary, and adds at most 1% parameters. The head is discarded after training, so inference remains unchanged. We compare LightMTP with NTP, three token-level MTP methods, and a latent MTP baseline at model sizes up to 1.5B parameters. LightMTP produces hidden states that are substantially more predictive of future tokens than NTP, while incurring a smaller next-token performance cost than the MTP baselines. On Countdown, a planning task, LightMTP improves over NTP by up to 10 points and more often preserves target reachability. On code generation and math reasoning, it follows output formats more consistently and makes fewer structural errors than NTP. Unlike the token-level MTP baselines, LightMTP also retains NTP-level performance on general language modeling benchmarks, supporting its use as a general-purpose pretraining objective. 

2 RELATED WORK 

NTP vs MTP. Predicting several future tokens predates decoder-only LLMs, having been explored both to accelerate decoding (Stern et al., 2018) and as a pretraining objective in ProphetNet (Qi et al., 2020) to reduce local overfitting. Gloeckle et al. (2024) first applied MTP to decoder-only transformer models by assigning each future token position its own head, with all positions sharing the unembedding matrix and transformer’s trunk. For simplicity, their method does not fully model the joint probability of the next k tokens but approximates it by assuming conditional independence 2given the previous context. This led Basharin et al. (2025) to propose using a rank-r probability decomposition to better approximate the joint future token distribution, subsequently achieving lower joint token loss on one dataset. However, they do not demonstrate the impact on more general pretraining benchmarks. The first to pretrain a large-scale frontier model with MTP, DeepSeek-v3 models the joint token probability via sequential per-position modules that partially share parameters (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025). However, they only train with one additional token ( k = 2 ). While it has been shown that LLMs plan internally for some future tokens beyond the immediate next one (Pal et al., 2023), this effect is limited and might benefit from more explicit supervision during training. 

Latent MTP. Latent MTP methods differ mainly in where their targets come from. Semformer (Yin et al., 2024) trains planning tokens to predict an autoencoder’s representation of the response, next-concept prediction (Liu et al., 2026) predicts vector-quantized concepts spanning several tokens, and Future Summaries (Mahajan et al., 2026) predicts the hidden states of a separately pretrained right-to-left language model. These targets require auxiliary encoders, codebooks, or helper models, which add parameters and training stages. Similar to our work, NextLat (Teoh et al., 2026) also regress the model’s own stop-gradient hidden states. They train a transition model that must predict each next hidden state from only the current state and the next token to obtain more compact, less shortcut-prone representations that generalize and plan better. LightMTP differs in three ways. It predicts states up to k steps ahead from the current state alone, without access to the intervening ground-truth tokens, learning to anticipate rather than model transitions. Furthermore, it predicts the future in a single parallel head call rather than a sequential rollout. Lastly, it takes its targets from an intermediate layer and uses only a regression loss, avoiding any projection onto the vocabulary.

We now describe the architecture of LightMTP. 3.1 LATENT PREDICTION HEAD AND TRAINING OBJECTIVE 

A transformer trunk with L layers maps a prefix x ≤t to hidden states hlt for l = 1 , . . . , L . Standard NTP trains a linear head M ∈ R|V |× d on hLt with a cross-entropy loss LNTP . As visualized in Figure 1, we add a second head gϕ, parametrized separately from the trunk parameters θ, which reads 

hLt and predicts hidden states the trunk itself produces at future positions. During training, this head shapes the transformer trunk’s parameters that are shared with the NTP objective, leading to hidden states that are more informed about future tokens. In our experiments, gϕ is a two-layer MLP with a GELU nonlinearity.

5 RESULTS 

We organize the results around the cost of better future token representation (§ 5.1), general language modeling benchmarks (§ 5.2), planning (§ 5.3), and reasoning and coding (§ 5.4) abilities. 5.1 FUTURE TOKEN ANTICIPATION 

LightMTP, as well as the other MTP approaches, adds an explicit local lookahead pressure to the pretraining objective. To measure how this additional objective affects the models’ representations, we train linear probes to predict the token at t + k using the models’ frozen last hidden-layer outputs on a held-out FineWeb-Edu shard. Since the last hidden layer directly receives gradients from the MTP heads, we expect MTP models to surface more future-token information in this layer, making future tokens more easily linearly decodable. The resulting probing results, shown in Appendix B.1, reveal a tradeoff: MTP methods generally improve future-token prediction ( k ≥ 2), but this comes at a cost to NTP ( k = 1 ), most prominently for Meta’s and DeepSeek’s MTP approaches. Variance-based regularization further improves future-token anticipation for LightMTP, but also increases this cost.

We expect MTP training to improve planning, as it encourages models to consider longer horizons. In Countdown (Gandhi et al., 2024), players combine four numbers via basic arithmetic operators (+, -, ×, ÷) to arrive at a target number. Countdown requires models to explore the search space spanned by the numbers and operators, with which NTP has been shown to struggle. We follow Teoh et al. (2026) to generate data where targets ∈ [10 , 100] . We define accuracy as the ratio of arithmetically correct answers that consume each input number exactly once and end on the target. 3

On the held-out Countdown targets, both LightMTP variants substantially outperform the NTP baseline and improve consistently as the lookahead horizon k increases . LightMTP-S outperforms LightMTP-C at every k and does so with lower variance at the largest horizon. Notably, it achieves this with only 1.2M MTP parameters, compared to 75 M for the Meta-L objective: a 98% reduction in parameter count. Since for k=1 the Meta models are identical to an NTP model, they cannot offer gains at this horizon. Only Meta-L surpasses LightMTP at k ≥ 4, reaching its own peak at k=8 . The DeepSeek MTP objective peaks earlier, at k=4 , then declines at k=8 , the only method for which additional lookahead is counterproductive. To further investigate where MTP’s edge comes from, we quantify the models’ error types in Figure 5. Each failing completion was classified by the raised exception type. We re-port structural errors , consisting of NameError , SyntaxError , IndentationError , and 

ModuleNotFoundError , i.e., failures that are attributable to local naming/syntax consistency rather than task or data understanding. 

Compared to NTP, all MTP models exhibit fewer structural errors , to a minimal degree even DeepSeek, which underperformed NTP on task performance by a large margin. Fewer structural errors support our hypothesis that MTP objectives teach local look-ahead skills, thus benefiting local coherence but not necessarily underlying task understanding. Future Summaries benefits from the largest reduction in structure-related errors, followed by the two LightMTP variants, and a more moderate effect on Meta and Meta-L. For some qualitative examples of structure-related generation differences between NTP and LightMTP, see Appendix B.6. 96 CONCLUSION 

This paper introduces LightMTP, a latent multi-token prediction objective using a model’s own future hidden states for supervision via a small auxiliary head. By predicting in latent space rather than over the vocabulary, it avoids the compute and memory overhead of token-level MTP, adding at most 1% extra parameters and leaving the trunk and the inference path untouched. Across probing, planning, reasoning, and code generation, LightMTP balances between anticipating future tokens and retaining next-token performance better than all considered MTP baselines. This balance is particularly relevant at smaller model scales ( < 3B), a regime for which prior work either reports degraded language modeling or that it omits entirely: LightMTP improves at modeling local task structure while leaving general language modeling benchmarks largely unaffected, making it practical as a pretraining objective where existing MTP methods are not. Future work characterizing the choices affecting the anticipation cost systematically, across target granularity, prediction horizon, and model scale, would provide a principled basis for designing multi-token objectives rather than selecting them empirically. 

ETHICS STATEMENT 

This work proposes a pretraining objective for language models and evaluates it on publicly available data. It involves no human subjects, crowdsourcing, or personally collected data. We pretrain on FineWeb-Edu, a filtered subset of CommonCrawl, and, for the coding experiments, on the Python subset of The Stack v1.2, which contains only permissively licensed code and honors developer opt-out requests. We evaluate on established public benchmarks (CORE, LAMBADA, GSM8K, MBPP) and on synthetically generated Countdown problems. Models trained on web-scale corpora may inherit the biases, factual errors, and harmful content in that data. Our models are small ( ≤1.5B parameters), are not instruction-tuned, and were trained solely for research, so we consider their potential for misuse to be low. Training the models reported here required a substantial number of GPU hours, and we report all training budgets in Appendix 6 to support reproducibility.

A.1 ARCHITECTURE AND HYPERPARAMETERS 

All models build on the nanochat codebase, a compact reimplementation of a modern GPT-style decoder. We keep its architecture and training recipe unchanged across all methods and did not tune any hyperparameter for LightMTP or the baselines. Table 5 lists the settings for the 24-layer models; the 12- and 18-layer models differ only in depth and in the depth-derived quantities (width, heads, weight decay, training steps). 

Architecture. Width scales with depth at a fixed aspect ratio ( dmodel = 64 · L) with 128-dimensional attention heads. Each block is a pre-norm Transformer block with parameter-free RMSNorm, no bias terms, rotary position embeddings (base 10 4) followed by QK-normalization, and a 4× MLP with a squared-ReLU activation. The token embedding is normalized before the first block and the final hidden state before the output head. Input embedding and output head are untied, and output logits are soft-capped as 15 tanh( z/ 15) . Attention alternates between local windows (“S”, half the context) and full context (“L”) in a repeating “SSSL” pattern, with the last layer always using full context. Two further additions are learnable per-layer scalars that rescale the residual stream and mix the normalized input embedding x0 back in, x ← λres

x0, and ResFormer-style value embeddings: in every other layer (always including the last), a separate token-embedding table is added to the attention values through an input-dependent gate. 

Optimization. Following nanochat , all 2D weight matrices inside the Transformer blocks are optimized with Muon, using Nesterov momentum, Polar-Express orthogonalization (5 iterations), per-neuron second-moment normalization, and cautious weight decay. Embeddings, value embeddings, the output head and the per-layer scalars are optimized with AdamW. The learning rate is held constant for the first half of training and then decays linearly to zero. Muon momentum warms up from 0.85 to 0.95 over the first 300 steps, and weight decay decays linearly to zero over training. Training uses bfloat16 autocast (logits and loss in fp32), torch.compile , and FlashAttention-3 on Hopper GPUs. The auxiliary heads of all MTP methods are 2D matrices and are therefore optimized with Muon under the same settings as the trunk. 

Data and tokenization. We use nanochat ’s byte-level BPE tokenizer with a vocabulary of 

215 =32 ,768 tokens and a GPT-4-style pre-tokenization pattern (digits grouped in runs of at most two), trained on 2B characters of FineWeb-Edu. Sequences of 2,048 tokens are packed with a BOS-aligned best-fit strategy: every row starts at a document boundary and is filled without padding by the largest documents that fit, cropping one document to fill any remaining space. Attention is causal over the packed row, while the LightMTP targets are masked so that they never cross a document boundary (Section 3.3). 

Training budget. Following nanochat ’s speedrun recipe, the number of optimizer steps is set so that the number of training tokens is 12 times the number of scaling parameters (Transformer matrices plus output head; embeddings excluded). Meta-L. The k−1 auxiliary heads are untied linear LM heads, each an independent copy of the unembedding matrix, reading the same final hidden state. This variant is also presented in Gloeckle et al. (2024), in the alternative-architecture ablation of their appendix, where heads that are a single linear layer without nonlinearity are compared against the transformer-layer heads of the main architecture. The authors report no significant improvement from the alternatives. We keep it as a separate baseline because it isolates the effect of separate unembedding matrices. 

DeepSeek-v3-style MTP. DeepSeek-V3 (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025) augments next-token prediction with D = k − 1 sequential MTP modules that preserve a causal chain across the predicted positions. Each module applies RMSNorm to the previous depth’s representation hk−1 

> i

and to the embedding of token ti+k, concatenates the two, projects the result with Mk ∈ Rd×2d, and passes it through its own Transformer block. The embedding table and the output head are shared with the main model, so each module adds only Mk and one block. The per-depth cross-entropy losses are averaged and added to the NTP loss with weight λ. We keep this structure and adapt two settings to our comparison. First, we use D = k − 1 = 3 modules rather than the D = 1 of DeepSeek-V3, so that the objective supervises the same four positions as the other methods. Second, we set λ = 1 , as for every other method, rather than DeepSeek-V3’s schedule of 0.3 decaying to 0.1, since we do not tune loss weights per method. Each module’s transformer block is randomly initialized and inherits the local attention window of our backbone’s first layer (1,024 tokens) rather than attending over the full context. As in the original, the modules are discarded at inference. At depth 24 they add 99M parameters (Table 6). 

Future Summaries. Future Summary Prediction (Mahajan et al., 2026) trains an auxiliary head to predict a compact representation of the entire future continuation rather than individual future tokens. We reproduce their learned-summary variant: a separate reverse language model is pretrained right-to-left on the same corpus, and the left-to-right model is then trained to predict that model’s hidden state from its own. The reverse model is frozen throughout and is never used at inference. As in the original, we treat its pretraining as a one-time overhead and mostly exclude it from the token budgets in Table 6. It has the same depth, width and vocabulary as the model it supervises, so it roughly doubles the total pretraining compute of this baseline. We deviate in three aspects. First, we implement only the learned-summary variant, not the handcrafted bag-of-words summaries the original also proposes. Second, the auxiliary head is the same two-layer MLP with a GELU nonlinearity that LightMTP uses, rather than the transformer block of the original, so that the two latent methods are compared at equal head capacity. Third, we use a cosine loss on unit-normalized vectors rather than the original’s ℓ2 loss, again matching LightMTP. Targets are taken from the reverse model’s final layer and predicted from the final layer of the left-to-right trunk. At depth 24 the head adds 4.7M parameters, the same as LightMTP single-target.

B.3 COUNTDOWN 

Countdown problems are generated following Teoh et al. (2026): We reserve 10% of the targets for out-of-distribution evaluation, so held-out problems have targets the model never saw during training. We generate 500,000 training problems, 10,000 in-distribution, and 10,000 held-out-target evaluation problems, each with four input numbers and a three-equation solution. The in-distribution evaluation set is deduplicated against the training set by exact string match, the held-out-target set is distinct by construction. Every generated solution is verified to be arithmetically valid and to reach its target. Countdown uses its own tokenizer, in which each integer below 10,000 is a single token, and eight pause tokens (Goyal et al., 2024) separate the problem from the solution. B.4 GSM8K For GSM8K, we evaluate on the 1,319-example test split, with eight in-context examples sam-pled per test item from the official 7,473-example training split. Exemplars are rendered as Q: {question}\nA: {rationale} The answer is {answer}. , and examples are gradu-ally dropped if a prompt exceeds the context window. B.5 MBPP MBPP uses the official task-ID splits: tasks 601–974 for finetuning, tasks 511–600 for evaluation, and tasks 2–4 from the reserved prompt split as few-shot exemplars, so the exemplars never overlap the evaluation set. Prompts follow the original MBPP format (problem statement, the three assert statements, and a [BEGIN] /[DONE] delimited solution). Generated code is cut at [DONE] and executed in a sandbox against the problem’s asserts, with a five-second timeout.
