Abstract 

Open coding agents are commonly trained through supervised fine-tuning on interaction trajectories whose final patches pass the bench-mark test suite. However, this outcome-level fil-tering criterion ignores process quality: trajec-tories that eventually succeed may still contain inefficient interaction patterns such as repet-itive file inspection, unstable tool usage, or delayed modification behavior, all of which become part of the imitation target. We pro-pose TQS (Trajectory Quality Score), a struc-tural scoring framework for coding-agent tra-jectories that operates directly on trajectory structure, requires no LLM judge, and runs in linear time. TQS characterizes trajectory quality through structural signals organized around structured convergence: information focus, intent–action alignment, and policy dy-namics. Using 155,144 successful trajecto-ries from a public open-license corpus, we rank trajectories by TQS and fine-tune coding agents on filtered subsets. Models trained on top-ranked trajectories consistently outperform those trained on random or bottom-ranked sub-sets on SWE-Bench Verified. Our results sug-gest that process-level trajectory quality is an important factor in coding-agent supervision and that structural trajectory filtering provides an effective and scalable alternative to LLM-based data selection. 1 Introduction 

Coding agents that interact with sandboxed en-vironments to inspect repositories, execute tests, and edit files have recently achieved strong per-formance on SWE-Bench Verified-style software engineering tasks (Jimenez et al., 2024; OpenAI, 2024). Beyond advances in foundation models and inference-time scaffolding, an increasingly impor-tant ingredient in these systems is trajectory super-vision: training on multi-turn agent demonstrations consisting of reasoning traces, tool calls, tool ob-servations, and final patches (Pan et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2024; Ariyak et al., 2026). For medium-scale open-weight models, a common and effective practice is to collect such trajectories from more capable teacher agents and retain those whose final patches pass the benchmark test suite. However, outcome-level correctness does not necessarily imply process-level quality. Among successful trajectories, behavioral patterns vary substantially. Some trajectories localize the bug quickly, maintain coherent search scope, and tran-sition efficiently from exploration to modification. Others repeatedly inspect the same files, interrupt stalled shell commands, or postpone meaningful edits until late in the interaction budget, yet still eventually reach a passing patch through extended trial-and-error. These differences matter because trajectory-level supervised fine-tuning exposes the student to the full interaction process, including rea-soning traces, tool use, and the final patch (Kamoi et al., 2024). Inefficient or unstable interaction pat-terns therefore become part of the training signal. The instruction-tuning literature has shown that curated subsets can outperform larger, noisier train-ing corpora (Zhou et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024b; Xia et al., 2024b). Coding-agent trajectories pose a different selec-tion problem. They are long interaction histories whose ordering records exploration, editing, test-ing, and recovery. Perplexity-style scores are con-founded by length and tokenization, while LLM-based judges are costly at the scale of hundreds of thousands of trajectories and billions of tokens. Furthermore, these approaches are not designed to characterize how effectively an agent organizes its search process over time. In this work, we propose TQS (Trajectory Qual-ity Score), a structural scoring framework for OpenHands-style coding-agent trajectories. The agent should focus its observations and file operations on task-relevant evidence, keep its stated intent aligned with its tool actions, and move from broad exploration toward productive edit, test, and recovery behavior. We operationalize these lev-els through seven trajectory-level measures, each computable in linear time from event logs alone. Empirically, we score 155,144 successful tra-jectories from CoderForge-Preview (Ariyak et al., 2026) and construct matched top-ranked, bottom-ranked, and random subsets from the eligible fixed-budget SFT pool under task- and repository-level diversity constraints. We LoRA-fine-tune the same Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct model (Ariyak et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2025a; Hu et al., 2022) on each subset using identical hyperparameters. Mod-els trained on top-ranked trajectories consistently outperform those trained on random or bottom-ranked subsets on SWE-Bench Verified (OpenAI, 2024) under the OpenHands framework (Wang et al., 2025). These results suggest that trajectory-level process structure is an important and previ-ously underexplored dimension of coding-agent supervision. Our contributions are threefold: (i) we formulate coding-agent trajectory quality through structured convergence and operationalize it with seven effi-cient trajectory-level measures; (ii) we introduce a scalable framework for trajectory scoring and fil-tering without relying on LLM judges or external reward models; and (iii) we present an end-to-end open pipeline for trajectory scoring, filtering, fine-tuning, and evaluation, demonstrating that quality-aware trajectory selection improves downstream coding-agent performance. 

2 Related Work 

Coding agents and trajectory supervision. Re-cent coding agents interact with repositories through tool-augmented environments that sup-port file inspection, shell execution, testing, and patch generation. Frameworks such as SWE-agent (Yang et al., 2024) and OpenHands (Wang et al., 2025) standardized the agent-computer in-teraction pipeline and enabled large-scale collec-tion of executable agent trajectories. Building on these infrastructures, datasets such as SWE-Gym (Pan et al., 2025), Lingma SWE-GPT (Ma et al., 2024), and CoderForge-Preview (Ariyak et al., 2026) progressively scaled teacher-generated trajectory supervision for open-weight coding agents. Recent work has also explored trajec-tory synthesis and reward-guided curation for soft-ware and GUI agents (Sun et al., 2025). While reinforcement-learning approaches for software en-gineering agents (Wei et al., 2025) further improve exploration and decision-making, they typically still rely on strong supervised initialization, mak-ing trajectory quality important for both SFT and RL-based pipelines. 

Data selection for supervised fine-tuning. Data-selection methods for supervised fine-tuning usu-ally use an external judge or a training-derived importance signal. AlpaGasus (Chen et al., 2024) and DEITA (Liu et al., 2024) ask stronger LLMs to score samples. LESS (Xia et al., 2024b), Cherry-LLM (Li et al., 2024b), and Superfiltering (Li et al., 2024a) estimate utility through gradient influence or model-internal signals; related methods have also been applied to code instruction tuning (Wang et al., 2024). These selectors were developed for short, single-turn samples. Applying them to an agent trajectory would require redefining their sample-level signals over a long interaction history.  

> TQS

instead uses the temporal structure already present in the event log. 

Process-level evaluation and trajectory quality. 

Recent work has increasingly emphasized evalu-ating intermediate reasoning and interaction pro-cesses rather than final outcomes alone. Prior studies on chain-of-thought faithfulness analyze whether intermediate reasoning traces are consis-tent with the eventual answer (Lanham et al., 2023; Bentham et al., 2024). AgentDiagnose (Ou et al., 2025) applies an LLM rubric to browser-agent trajectories and shows that process-aware trajec-tory selection can improve training efficiency. Be-yond this, several methods use entropy or behav-ioral diversity to analyze and optimize agent poli-cies (Chen et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Dong et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025a). Recent work has also ex-plored process supervision and trajectory curation for software-engineering agents using privileged patch-level information (Ma et al., 2026).

3.1 Preliminaries and Notation 

A trajectory τ = ( s0, s 1, . . . , s n−1) is a sequence of n steps. Each step is represented as 

st = ( at, m t, o t, F t, ε t),

where at ∈ A is an action type (one of think ,

read_file , write_file , execute_bash , search ,

submit , other ); mt is the free-text thought that precedes the action; ot is the tool observation re-turned after the action; Ft ⊆ F is the set of file paths touched by the action; and εt ∈ { 0, 1} indi-cates whether ot matches a predefined error pattern. This tuple is recoverable from any OpenHands-style event log without model inference. 

3.2 Design Intuition and Score Construction 

A coding-agent trajectory is a search process over observations, files, actions, and intermediate hy-potheses. We view a high-quality trajectory as one that exhibits structured convergence : over the course of the interaction, the search becomes or-ganized around the emerging fix. TQS relies on event-log evidence that the agent is narrowing its search toward a task-relevant region. At minimum, it provides a low-cost way to filter out clearly un-desirable trajectory patterns. Structured convergence has three observable lev-els. (A) Information focus asks whether the agent narrows the relevant evidence through its observa-tions and file operations. (B) Intent–action align-ment asks whether the stated thought before a tool call points in the same direction as the action that follows. (C) Policy dynamics asks whether the interaction policy develops a productive rhythm of exploration, execution, and error recovery. Some failures are categorical rather than gradual; the tra-jectories associated with them are inefficient. A run may trigger an early-stop pattern, never make a sub-stantive edit, or repeat the same failing command.

3.4 Discriminant Validity and External Sanity Checks 

We use task outcome as a weak admission signal for candidate components. Ineffective process pat-terns often make failure more likely, so a useful component should tend to rank a passing trajectory above a failing trajectory from the same task. This comparison filters signals with the wrong direction or strong confounds. Once the components are fixed, TQS ranks only task-successful trajectories for training, using event-log structure alone and no further patch-level supervision. Concretely, we pair each passing trajectory with a failing trajectory from the same task. For each candidate measure, we compute two quantities. The first is concordance: the rate at which the measure assigns a higher score to the passing tra-jectory than to its paired failing trajectory, with 0.5 corresponding to chance. The second is the absolute Spearman correlation |ρ| with trajectory length, which serves as a simple check against length-driven proxies. A measure is admitted only if its concordance exceeds 0.5 and |ρ| < 0.25 .Table 1 summarizes the results. The seven retained components pass both criteria, and the composite TQS reaches a concordance of 0.732, above every individual component. 5 Results 

5.1 Main Results 

Table 3 reports the main results. Under the same backbone, SFT recipe, and evaluation scaffold, the selected trajectories have a clear effect on down-stream performance. Random-K improves only slightly over the base model, from 24.4 to 24.8 pass@1. In contrast, TQS -Top reaches 27.6, im-proving over Random-K by 2.8 points and over the base model by 3.2 points. Bottom-K moves in the opposite direction, dropping to 21.8. This order-ing shows that trajectories with the same reward-1 outcome can differ substantially in their value as supervision. 6 Conclusion 

"Passing" is too coarse a quality signal for coding-agent SFT data. We introduced TQS , a lightweight event-log scorer that evaluates successful trajecto-ries through information focus, intent–action align-ment, policy dynamics, and a guardrail for categor-ical failure patterns. Across our experiments, high- 

> TQS

trajectories provide more effective supervision than simpler outcome-, length-, or pattern-based selection, while low-TQS trajectories can even hurt downstream performance. At the same time, TQS 

approaches the effectiveness of rubric-based GPT-4o judging at substantially lower selection cost, making repeated rescoring and corpus-scale data selection practical. These results suggest that suc-cessful trajectories are not equally valuable as su-pervision, and that trajectory structure offers a use-ful, scalable signal beyond task outcome alone. 

Limitations 

Our experiments use CoderForge-Preview trajec-tories generated by a single teacher model under the OpenHands scaffold. Although the compo-nents rely on general event-log fields, their parsers and thresholds may require recalibration for dif-ferent action spaces, multilingual traces, or other step granularities. Transfer to GUI or multimodal agents would also require new definitions of scope and intent. Outside the current setting, the hand-set component weights may likewise require recalibra-tion. TQS also scores the structure of an observed process rather than verifying whether a passing patch addresses the underlying root cause. Our SFT experiments focus on a fixed-budget setting with 1,024 selected trajectories per condi-tion. We also restrict training trajectories to at most 32K tokens so that they fit within the 40,960-token context after template overhead. This excludes a long-trajectory tail that may contain useful recov-ery behavior. We train each condition once because of compute cost, so the reported pass@1 differ-ences do not include multi-seed variance estimates. Our downstream evaluation is limited in model scale. Preliminary experiments with a 7B back-bone did not show stable selector differences, so we focus the reported SFT comparison on the 30B model. Larger backbones remain untested.

Scoring cost. The scorer is a deterministic CPU pipeline, and each component is linear in trajectory length. We benchmark the current implementation on an AMD EPYC 7413 using 3,462 mixed-source trajectories with a median length of 53 steps. A single worker processes this batch in 149.2 sec-onds, or 23.2 trajectories per second (43 ms per trajectory). At this rate, scoring all 258,134 unique trajectories requires about 3.1 CPU-hours. Four workers reach 87.5 trajectories per second, which corresponds to about 49 minutes for a full-corpus pass. Profiling attributes 58% of the single-worker runtime to regular-expression error detection and 13% to the character-level entropy calculation used by OEC .

Loss masking. For SFT, ms-swift computes loss on assistant responses, including tool calls, while masking user messages and tool responses. Under the Qwen3-Coder agent template, tool calls are rendered in XML. For example: 

<tool_call> <function=execute_bash> <parameter=command> pytest tests/ </parameter> </function> </tool_call> 

This setup trains the model to imitate both natural-language reasoning and tool invocation, while ex-cluding user messages and tool observations from the supervised loss. As a result, the loss focuses on the agent’s reasoning and actions, while tool outputs serve only as conditioning context. 

Error flags. The binary error flag εt is set from tool observations using a small set of string and regular-expression patterns, including common ex-ecution failures, missing-file messages, traceback markers, failed test invocations, and tool-level error statuses. The flag is intentionally conservative: it is used to detect repeated failure patterns and post-error behavior, not to infer semantic correctness of the final patch. 

C.1 Subset Sampler 

We construct fixed-budget training subsets from the eligible pool described in §3.3. Each subset contains 1,024 trajectories. Top-K and Bottom-

K are selected by descending and ascending TQS ,respectively, while Random-K is sampled with a fixed seed. To keep the comparison focused on tra-jectory quality, the sampler controls three sources of variation: task identity, repository coverage, and source provenance. For Top-K and Bottom-K, we first sort eligible trajectories by TQS in the corresponding direction. The sampler then fills the subset greedily. In the first pass, it selects at most one trajectory per task. In the second pass, it prioritizes trajectories from repositories not yet represented in the subset. In the final pass, it fills any remaining slots in score order, subject to a hard per-task cap of 30 trajectories. This preserves TQS ranking as the primary selection criterion while avoiding subsets dominated by a small number of tasks or repositories. E Failed Candidate Measures 

We also considered several candidate measures that were motivated by the same structured-convergence view but failed the admission protocol in §3.4. Their definitions are included here to clarify the boundary of the final score. 

HCR (Hypothesis–Commit Ratio). HCR char-acterizes how an agent allocates steps between ex-ploration and execution. We define exploration as 

R = {think , read_file , search } and execution as E = {write_file , execute_bash }, and com-pute their ratio within the early, middle, and late thirds of the trajectory: 

Rg = #{t ∈ Gg : at ∈ R} 

#{t ∈ Gg : at ∈ E} .

HCR assigns score 1 when Rearly ≥ Rmid ≥ Rlate ,favoring an explore-then-commit pattern, and 0 oth-erwise. Although it passes the numerical thresholds (concordance 0.527, |ρ| = 0 .014 ), including HCR caused 99.9% of the Top-1K subset to share the same binary pattern, so we exclude it under the behavioral-diversity criterion. 

BUS (Backtracking Utility Score). BUS distin-guishes productive redirection from unproductive wandering. A backtracking event is detected when the agent’s current target-file set has no overlap with that of the preceding k steps. The event is considered useful if, within the following k steps, the search scope narrows or the agent makes a sub-stantive edit: 

sBUS = # useful backtracking events 

# backtracking events .

Higher BUS therefore indicates that changes in direction more often lead to focused progress. Its concordance is only 0.202, so we exclude it. 

TGCR (Tool-Graph Cycle Ratio). TGCR cap-tures structural redundancy in file navigation. We construct a directed graph whose nodes are files and whose edges represent successive file-access transitions, and use DFS coloring to identify back edges: 

sTGCR = # back edges 

# total edges .A larger value indicates more cyclic revisitation, whereas a trajectory closer to a DAG reflects a cleaner access path. TGCR obtains concordance 0.479, close to chance, and is therefore excluded. 

DCS-Jaccard (Decision Consistency Score). 

DCS measures local consistency between reason-ing, action, and subsequent feedback. For each step, it combines intent–action alignment with the extent to which the resulting observation informs the next intent:

DCS-Jaccard instantiates sim( ·, ·) with Jaccard similarity and averages the resulting scores over valid steps. Although intended to capture coher-ent local transitions, it has |ρ| = 0 .729 with step count and is therefore rejected as strongly length-confounded. 

DCS-file (file-alignment variant). DCS-file re-places lexical Jaccard overlap with file-level align-ment when evaluating the same intent–action and observation–intent transitions. It therefore asks whether the files implied by the agent’s reasoning agree with those actually acted on and whether observed files are reflected in the next plan. The resulting score obtains concordance 0.238, substan-tially below chance in the intended direction, and is excluded. 

F LLM-Judge Baseline Rubric 

The B5 baseline uses GPT-4o as a trajectory quality judge under an AgentDiagnose-style rubric. The judge sees the issue description, the trajectory’s rea-soning and tool-call sequence, and the four rubric dimensions below. It does not see the reference patch or held-out test outcome. Each dimension is scored on a 1–5 Likert scale, and the trajectory score is the mean of the four dimensions. The top 1,024 trajectories by mean score, after the same eligibility filters and subset constraints as Top-K,form the B5 training subset.
