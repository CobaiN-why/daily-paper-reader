# When Sub-Agents Work in Parallel: The Promises and Pitfalls of Dynamic Concurrency in Long-Horizon Coding Tasks 

HAN LI, Nanjing University, China 

HANHAONING LI, Central University of Finance and Economics, China 

ZIQIAN JIANG, Nanjing University, China 

YILING LOU, University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, USA As coding agents advance toward increasingly complex and long-horizon software development tasks, dynamic concurrency has emerged as a promising mechanism for scaling agent execution. Unlike predefined multi-agent workflows, dynamic concurrency allows a main agent to decide during execution whether, when, and how to spawn and coordinate concurrent sub-agents. However, its effectiveness, efficiency, and failure characteristics remain poorly understood, particularly for long-horizon coding tasks. In this work, we present the first systematic study of native dynamic concurrency in three frontier coding agents: Codex, Claude Code, and Kimi Code. We conduct controlled comparisons with dynamic concurrency enabled or disabled across 354 tasks from four long-horizon benchmarks and one bounded benchmark, yielding 2,124 executions and costing over $20K. Our results show that dynamic concurrency benefits most on long-horizon tasks, while bounded tasks often see little or negative improvement. Meanwhile, concurrency substantially increases resource consumption, with token usage reaching 1.41–3.31 times that of sequential execution. Through trajectory analysis, we develop a taxonomy of concurrency failure patterns comprising four main categories, 13 subcategories, and 28 specific patterns. Finally, we identify conditions under which concurrency provides concrete benefits, including complementary parallel work, alternative solution exploration, and independent validation. Together, our findings reveal both the promise and limitations of dynamic concurrency and provide empirical guidance for enabling more effective dynamic collaboration among multiple sub-agents. 1 Introduction 

The recent advances of Large Language Models (LLMs) have enabled a remarkable progress in LLM-based coding agents [34, 62], which demonstrate increasingly strong capabilities across a broad range of critical software engineering tasks, from code generation [11] and debugging to repository-level issue resolution. As these agents are applied to increasingly complex and long-horizon tasks, however, a single agent often faces substantial limitations in its context, reasoning, and execution capacity. In particular, long-horizon software development may involve extensive code exploration, implementation, testing, and verification [5] over prolonged trajectories, motivating the need to scale agent execution beyond a single sequential trajectory [17, 63]. Multi-agent systems provide a promising path toward such scaling. By decomposing a complex task into subtasks and delegating them to multiple sub-agents, these approaches can increase execution capacity, enable parallel exploration of alternative solutions, and reduce end-to-end latency. Recent studies and agent systems have demonstrated the potential of multi-agent collab-oration and parallel execution across a variety of complex reasoning and software engineering

In fact, the earlier coding agents (e.g., TRAE [55] and OpenHands [58]) often adopt a static concurrency paradigm, which rely on predefined workflows and dedicated orchestration harnesses to coordinate the collaboration between sub-agents. For example, the number and roles of sub-agents, their task assignments, communication patterns, and synchronization points are often specified in advance for a particular workflow. While effective in well-structured settings, such a static concurrency requires developers to anticipate how a task should be decomposed and parallelized before execution. This rigidity makes it difficult to accommodate diverse and evolving tasks whose decomposition, dependencies, and opportunities for parallelism may become apparent only as execution unfolds, limiting the flexibility and generality of these approaches. A recent trend among frontier coding agents is moving beyond such predefined parallel work-flows toward dynamic concurrency . Rather than following a fixed orchestration, the main agent dynamically determines during execution whether, when, and how to spawn concurrent sub-agents based on the evolving task state. Figure 1 illustrates the dynamic concurrency in three frontier coding agents (e.g., Codex multiagent Version2 [49], Claude Code Dynamic Workflow [2], and Kimi Code AgentSwarm [45]). With dynamic concurrency enabled, a main agent dynamically constructs an execution plan to orchestrate sub-agents while remaining active to collect their results, reconcile shared interfaces or files, and verify the integrated artifact. Sub-agents are instantiated and executed on demand according to instructions issued by the main agent. For example, in Claude Code, the main agent automatically generates a workflow script that schedules parallel stages for multiple sub-agents, specifies their explicit dependencies, and coordinates their joins. Similarly, in Kimi Code, the main agent launches a batch of sub-agent contexts instantiated from a shared template with task-specific assignments, waits for their completion, and returns an ordered report. In Codex, the main agent supports both direct and nested delegation of sub-agents while continuing its own execution and exchanging messages with sub-agents. While extensive efforts have evaluated coding agents and analyzed their failure patterns, existing studies have largely focused on either single-agent systems [3, 13, 25, 35, 66] or multi-agent systems organized around predefined collaboration protocols [10, 18, 22–24, 50, 56]. Moreover, existing evaluations predominantly target bounded coding tasks, such as SWE-bench [25, 60], where the expected outputs typically involve relatively small patches (e.g., only dozens of lines of code). In contrast, dynamic concurrency is particularly intended to support long-horizon and open-ended coding tasks, such as repository-level generation [17, 63, 67] and software release development [30], where the coding agents are expected to generate tens of thousands of lines of code per task. Therefore, it remains unclear how the emerging dynamic concurrency mechanisms in frontier coding agents affect their effectiveness and efficiency, particularly on long-horizon coding tasks. To fill this knowledge gap, we perform the first study on the native dynamic concurrency mech-anisms in frontier coding agents, including Codex multiagent Version2 [49], Claude Code Dynamic Workflow [2], and Kimi Code AgentSwarm [45]. In particular, we evaluate these coding agents with dynamic concurrency on a wide range of long-horizon coding tasks, including 226 repository generation tasks (e.g., RepoZero [67], ProgramBench [63], NL2Repo [17]) and 28 sustained reposi-tory evolution tasks (e.g., LoopsBench [30]). These long-horizon benchmarks span mean reference implementation or gold-change sizes of 0.8–54.4 thousand lines across different difficulty groups, providing broad implementation scopes for evaluating dynamic concurrency. To further enable a comparison between long-horizon coding tasks and bounded coding tasks, we also include 100 bounded coding tasks from the classic SWE-bench Verified [13]. In total, our evaluation yields 2,124 executions across a range of task complexities and execution horizons, with 11.2 billion recorded tokens corresponding to $20,780.02 at standard API rates. Built upon the evaluation results, we perform both quantitative and qualitative to analyze the promises and failure patterns of coding agents with dynamic concurrency in long horizon-coding tasks. In ( RQ1 ), we first systematically compare the effectiveness and efficiency of coding agents with dynamic concurrency or without dynamic concurrency (i.e., sequential mode). Interestingly, we find that dynamic concurrency does not consistently improve end-to-end task success rates, e.g., the resolution effects range from an 11.9 percentage point decrease to a 2.3 point increase, while mean token use reaches 1.41 to 3.31 times compared to the sequential mode. In particular, dynamic concurrency delivers pass rate gains on long-horizon benchmarks, especially LoopsBench, while providing no such gains on the bounded tasks (SWE-bench Verified). In ( RQ2 ), we further analyze the delegation and coordination behavior within each task progresses. In particular, we observe that dynamic concurrency is increasingly extended in more challenging tasks, e.g., the number of created sub-agents increases along with the task difficulty. Besides quantitative analysis, we examine common failure patterns in dynamic concurrency ( RQ3 ) and the mechanisms and conditions of observed benefits ( RQ4 ). By manually annotating complete execution trajectories (Cohen’s 𝜅 =

0.77 [15]), we develop a taxonomy with four main categories covering task orchestration, execution governance, global context management, and shared state and merge. These categories comprise 13 subcategories and 28 specific failure patterns. Moreover, we also find that dynamic concurrency brings benefits when sub-agents can complete complementary work, explore alternative solutions, or independently validate and correct implementation from the main agent. • Systematic Empirical Study. We systematically evaluate dynamic concurrency in frontier coding agents across long-horizon coding tasks, analyzing its effectiveness and efficiency, along with its sub-agent delegation and coordination behaviors.

2 Background and Related Work 

Concurrency in Coding Agents. Earlier coding agents organize agent collaboration through predefined roles and workflows, task graphs, or interaction protocols. For example, ChatDev and MetaGPT exemplify specialized roles and development workflows [22–24, 50]; CodeR coordinates issue resolution through predefined task graphs [10]. Multiagent Debate and SWE-Debate use repeated discussion rounds [18, 32, 52], while Mixture-of-Agents uses layered aggregation [56]. Different from the static paradigm, which orchestrates multiple sub-agents according to a predefined and fixed workflow, recent frontier coding agents propose dynamic concurrency for multi-agent collaboration. In this paradigm, the main coding agent dynamically decides during execution whether to spawn sub-agents, which tasks to delegate, and how to coordinate and integrate their results. In this work, we study the native dynamic-concurrency mechanisms of Codex Multiagent Version 2, Claude Code Dynamic Workflow, and Kimi Code AgentSwarm [2, 45, 49]. 

Empirical Studies of Coding Agents. To date, there have been extensive studies on coding agents. For example, several studies analyze coordination architectures and debate settings of coding agents, examining how interaction design affects accuracy, cost, and latency [14, 32, 54]. In addition, a substantial body of work has investigated failure modes in agents [36]. For example, TRAJEVAL exemplifies trajectory analyses of coding-agent errors and execution patterns associated with success or failure [4, 8, 12, 26, 35, 39, 41, 43]. Coordination studies, including MAST and AgentFail, examine inter-agent misalignment, information loss, and failure propagation [9, 37, 40, 51]. TrajAudit and CodeTracer exemplify automated diagnosis of coding-agent trajectories [33, 57], complementing broader work on failure attribution and feedback-based recovery [16, 20, 38, 64, 65, 68]. Different from existing work, our study focuses on dynamic concurrency, an emerging mechanism in frontier coding agents, and systematically investigates its behavior on challenging long-horizon coding tasks. In particular, we evaluate its effectiveness and efficiency at scale and characterize the failure patterns that arise when multiple sub-agents are coordinated via dynamic concurrency.

3 Methodology 3.1 Research Questions 

• RQ1: Effectiveness and Efficiency. How does enabling dynamic concurrency affect task outcomes and resource use across long-horizon coding tasks? 

• RQ2: Delegation and Coordination Behavior. How do frontier coding agents organize and coordinate concurrent work across sub-agents over time? 

• RQ3: Taxonomy of Concurrency Failure Patterns. What failure patterns arise in frontier coding agents using dynamic concurrency? 

• RQ4: Benefit Analysis of Dynamic Concurrency. Through what mechanisms and under what coordination conditions, does dynamic concurrency improve effectiveness or efficiency? 

3.2 Studied Agents 

We select three representative frontier coding agents that support native dynamic concurrency. As reinforcement learning during agentic post-training may adapt a model to its scaffold’s tool interfaces and interaction conventions [29], substituting another model could disrupt this compati-bility. We use agent–model configurations selected in July 2026, pairing Codex with GPT-5.4 [48], Claude Code with Claude Opus 5 [1], and Kimi Code with Kimi K3 [46]. Figure 1 illustrates their delegation and integration mechanisms. We use the native scaffold of all agents and request the highest reasoning effort. 

3.3 Benchmarks 

We mainly focus on long-horizon coding tasks as there is limited necessity for dispatching multiple sub-agents for simple and bounded tasks. To this end, we select four widely-used benchmarks for long-horizon software artifact construction and sustained software development, including (i) RepoZero , which include Py2JS tasks (i.e., reconstructing Python packages in JavaScript) and C2Rust tasks (i.e., reconstructing C interfaces in Rust) [67]; (ii) ProgramBench , which requires 6 Han Li et al. 

rebuilding complete command line programs from reference executables and documentation [63]; (iii) NL2Repo Bench , which requires constructing repositories from natural language specifications and an empty workspace [17]; and (iv) LoopsBench , which covers sustained work across dependent development units [30]. In addition, for comparison, we also include the classic coding benchmark 

SWE-bench Verified [13, 25], which requires to resolve one specific issue in an existing repository, as the representative of bounded and short-horizon coding tasks. Table 1 summarizes the statistics of studied tasks. The “Pop.” column refers to the number of tasks in the full benchmark. However, executing the entire long-horizon benchmarks with diverse settings can be extremely expensive (e.g., running the entire LoopsBench once can cost $11,354) given the massive tokens consumed for long horizon tasks. Therefore, for each benchmark, we make a representative sample of tasks across different difficulty groups (i.e., easy, medium, and hard). We adopt the original difficulty groups defined by each benchmark themselves. To ensure the sampled tasks still preserve each benchmark’s difficulty distribution, we use stratified random sampling for each difficulty group: we select 25% of tasks in each studied long-horizon benchmarks (i.e., RepoZero, ProgramBench, NL2Repo, and LoopsBench) and 20% of the tasks in the comparison benchmark SWE-bench Verified. The detailed sample distribution is presented in Table 1. Figure 3 further shows the implementation sizes (i.e., the lines of code of ground-truth outputs for each task) across different benchmarks and difficulty groups. Overall, the four studied long-horizon benchmarks require generating significantly larger scale of code than short-horizon tasks in SWE-bench Verified. In particular, LoopsBench stands as the even most complicated tasks (e.g., involving generating 26.8K lines of code) among all long-horizon benchmarks. In summary, our experiments include 354 distinct tasks, which are each executed with three coding agents against two settings (i.e., enable or disable dynamic concurrency), yielding 2,124 execution cells in total, with recorded token usage corresponding to an API cost of $20,780.02 at standard rates.

3.4 Experimental Process 

Each task is evaluated with all three coding agents in both modes, i.e., (i) concurrent mode which enables the dynamic concurrency, and (ii) sequential mode which disables the dynamic concurrency. For a fair comparison, we control key experimental conditions between two modes, including the backbone model, prompt, environment, and time budget. In particular, for each studied agent, we enable the dynamic concurrency with their native interface and default settings. Codex enables concurrency through multi_agent and multi_agent_v2 . Its sequential mode disables both op-tions. Claude Code uses ultracode to activate Dynamic Workflow. Its sequential mode disables Workflow, Task, and Agent tools. Kimi Code enables AgentSwarm with swarm_mode=true and When Sub-Agents Work in Parallel 7

uses swarm_mode=false in sequential mode. In particular, for all studied agents, disabling dynamic concurrency guarantees that no sub-agents will be created. After execution, we collect the complete trajectories of both the main agent and all sub-agents, the submitted artifacts, and official test evaluator results, to facilitate our further analysis.

Pass Rate Improvement. Dynamic concurrency shows promise in improving task and test pass rates in several long-horizon tasks, with the broadest gains on the most long-horizon benchmark LoopsBench. First, on LoopsBench, which contains the longest-horizon tasks among all studied benchmarks, dynamic concurrency improves task pass rates for Codex and Claude Code by up to 14.3 percentage points, while Kimi Code’s task pass rate remains unchanged. Test pass rates improve for all three agents, with gains of up to 6.0 percentage points. Second, the benefits are more mixed on the other long-horizon benchmarks. On NL2Repo, dynamic concurrency improves the task pass rates of Codex and Claude Code but decreases that of Kimi Code. On ProgramBench, which remains particularly challenging for all studied frontier coding agents (with a task pass rate of 0% across all agents), concurrency nevertheless improves partial progress, as measured by test pass rate, for Codex and Kimi Code. Substantial variation can arise even within the same benchmark. For example, Codex’s nearly unchanged aggregate task pass rate on RepoZero combines a decline on Py2JS (2.0/1.7 percentage-point decreases in task/test pass rates) with an improvement on C2Rust (6.0/2.4 percentage-point increases in task/test pass rates). The loss of two resolved Py2JS tasks and gain of three resolved C2Rust tasks largely offset each other, leaving just one additional resolved task out of 150 (40.0% to 40.7% overall). Third, dynamic concurrency provides no improvement on the bounded, short-horizon tasks in SWE-bench Verified. Moreover, Codex remains unchanged, while Claude Code and Kimi Code exhibit substantial decreases in pass rate. For Claude Code, its task pass rate decreases by 24.0 percentage points on SWE-bench Verified but increases by 14.3 percentage points on LoopsBench. In particular, pooling both task groups, we conduct exact McNemar tests [19, 42] with Holm correction [21] across the three agents and assess significance by comparing the adjusted 𝑝 -values against 𝛼 = 0.05. The overall declines for Claude Code ( 𝑝 = 5.08348 × 10 −7) and Kimi Code ( 𝑝 = 0.0132176) are statistically significant, whereas the improvement for Codex ( 𝑝 = 0.229481) is not. We further compare average test pass rates across the four long-horizon benchmarks, ordered by their mean implementation size (Figure 3). This measure also captures progress on tasks that are not fully solved. Averaged equally across the three agents, the difference between concurrent and sequential execution rises from −8.29 percentage points on RepoZero to −0.04 on NL2Repo, +1.24 on ProgramBench, and +4.31 on LoopsBench. The effectiveness difference across these benchmarks indicates that larger expected implementation sizes are associated with a greater average test pass advantage from dynamic concurrency.

4.2 Resource Efficiency 

As shown in Table 2, the column “Shell calls” includes the number of calls made by both the main agent and all sub-agents; the columns “Time” and “Token” present the agent execution time and overall costs based on recorded provider usage. 

Resource Efficiency. Overall, dynamic concurrency incurs substantially higher computational cost than sequential execution. As shown in Table 2, mean shell calls increase under concurrency for every agent and benchmark, often by several fold, reflecting the additional exploration and execution performed by dispatching multiple sub-agents. Token consumption similarly increases, reaching 1.41–3.31 × the sequential level across agents. More importantly, these additional resources do not translate proportionally into successful outcomes. When normalizing total token consumption across both successful and failed runs by the number of successfully resolved tasks, token usage per solved task increases for all three agents. Even Codex, the agent showing a small overall gain in resolved tasks, consumes 24.12 million recorded tokens per solved task under concurrency, compared with 7.69 million under sequential execution. These results indicate that the current concurrency mechanisms generally trade substantially greater resource consumption for limited aggregate effectiveness gains. 

Execution Time. Interestingly, despite parallel execution, dynamic concurrency does not generally reduce end-to-end runtime. Mean runtime increases for all three agents overall and in 14 of the 15 agent-benchmark combinations in Table 2. For example, Claude Code takes 27.8 minutes per SWE-bench Verified task under concurrency, compared with 17.5 minutes under sequential execution, despite resolving fewer tasks. Thus, the parallelism introduced by dynamic concurrency does not 10 Han Li et al. 

automatically translate into lower end-to-end latency. We will perform a qualitative analysis in RQ3 to investigate the root causes for additional latency from concurrent mode. The costs and time in Table 2 are calculated based on average over both successful and un-successful tasks. We further analyze the costs of the tasks that are successfully solved by both concurrent and sequential modes. Of the 366 cases solved in both modes, concurrent execution finishes faster in 102 cases (27.9%). In particular, in 46 cases (12.6%), concurrent mode completes the task substantially faster than sequential mode (at least 20% less time). The observation implies that meaningful speedups can be achieved from the concurrent mode when proper coordination and execution are scheduled. We will further perform a qualitative analysis in RQ4 to investigate the conditions underlying these successful speedup. 

Finding 2: Dynamic concurrency incurs substantially higher resource consumption and runtime on average, with limited returns in effectiveness. Nevertheless, it shows potential in achieving meaningful speedup (e.g., on 12.6% of tasks solved in both modes), highlighting opportunities for more selective and effective concurrency.

6 Taxonomy of Concurrency Failure Patterns (RQ3) 

This RQ manually investigates the common failure patterns in dynamic concurrency. 

6.1 Methodology 

We manually annotate all 1,062 execution trajectories generated by coding agents operating with dynamic concurrency. For each case, annotators review the complete concurrent trajectory alongside the corresponding sequential trajectory of the same coding agent on the same task, together with the execution outcomes and official test results from both runs. The review covers the main agent and all sub-agents (including nested ones), workflow and status records, and generated artifacts. Two annotators first analyze 50 trajectories to establish an initial codebook through open cod-ing [53]. They identify concrete coordination episodes and assign descriptive codes capturing observed behaviors and their execution- or artifact-level symptoms. From this analysis, we develop a taxonomy with three levels: main categories describe broad areas of coordination failure, subcate-gories distinguish forms of failure within each main category, and failure patterns capture their concrete manifestations. Three annotators participate in the formal annotation stage, with each case independently annotated by two of them using the codebook. For a case exhibiting concurrency failures, each annotator selects one or more applicable failure pattern labels and records the reasoning supporting the assignments. Cases with no identified failure pattern receive no label. Each failure pattern belongs to one subcategory within one main category, so the broader labels follow from the selected patterns without separate annotation decisions. We report a single inter-annotator agreement value for the independent failure pattern annotations (Cohen’s 𝜅 = 0.77) [15]. After independent annotation, the annotators discuss disagreements, and a senior author adjudicates any unresolved disputes.

A. Task Orchestration Problems. This category arises when the main agent fails to effectively divide and allocate work among itself and sub-agents. Specifically, required work may be left unassigned ( Missing Owner ), seemingly parallel execution may leave critical work sequential (Pseudo-Concurrency ), or uneven delegation may leave insufficient resources for critical work ( Load Imbalance ). Task orchestration accounts for 229 (28.5%) of all identified pattern instances. 

A1. Missing Owner. Some required work is never assigned to any agent, leaving part of the implementation or final assembly unfinished. In Kimi Code’s NL2Repo verifiers reconstruction, five sub-agents are assigned to parsers and rubrics, utilities and scripts, documentation, example environments, and a mock client, but no agent is assigned the core verifiers/envs module. One sub-agent later reports that the missing environment classes prevent end-to-end testing, and the submitted package passes none official tests. A2. Pseudo-Concurrency. Multiple sub-agents run in parallel, but the work that determines task completion remains sequential. For example, the main agent may parallelize investigation but wait for all investigation results before starting implementation. In Kimi Code’s ProgramBench tailspin reconstruction, 17 sub-agents concurrently investigate different behaviors of the log highlighter, but none is assigned to implementation. The main agent waits for their reports before starting implementation itself, and the execution ends during implementation planning without producing a working replacement, passing 0 of 785 tests. Thus, although many sub-agents run concurrently, the concurrency does not accelerate the critical implementation work. 

A3. Load Imbalance. This is the most frequent subcategory within task orchestration, accounting for 20.6% of all pattern instances and 39.8% of those identified for Claude Code. It occurs when too much effort is spent on some parallel activities, leaving insufficient time or resources for critical work, i.e., when the main agent launches too many sub-agents or assigns too much work to individual sub-agents. In Claude Code’s ProgramBench tailspin reconstruction, the main agent starts building a Rust scaffold while a workflow creates sub-agents to investigate ten behavior areas and launches additional critics. These investigations and repeated delegations continue until the deadline, when unfinished sub-agents are terminated without returning their results. The main agent is left with an incomplete crate that cannot build. Here, excessive investigation and delegation consume the execution budget needed to complete the implementation. 

B. Execution Governance Problems. Execution governance problems arise when the main agent fails to properly manage sub-agents after delegation. Specifically, sub-agent work may be handled at the wrong time ( Wrong Start, Join, or Result Timing ), failed work may be left unrecovered ( Failure Propagation ), or the main agent may fail to monitor progress and intervene when needed ( No Active Main Agent Monitoring ). 

B1. Wrong Start, Join, or Result Timing. This is the most frequent subcategory within execution governance. It occurs when sub-agent work starts, finishes, or is incorporated at the wrong time, causing required work or results to miss the final submission. A sub-agent may be terminated before finishing, finish too late for integration, or modify the artifact after verification. In the seqtk example (Figure 2), the main agent assumes a two-hour sub-agent timeout despite a 40-minute task budget. The task deadline therefore arrives before the planned join, terminating sub-agents with required work unfinished. Here, the delegation itself is reasonable, but its timing does not fit the available execution budget. 

B2. Failure Propagation. A sub-agent fails to complete its work, but the main agent neither retries, reassigns, nor takes over the unfinished work. In Claude Code’s RepoZero canonicaljson test3 migration, a sub-agent assigned to search for argument-parsing counterexamples is interrupted when a transient request spike triggers rate limiting, but the main agent does not restart it or take over its verification task before timeout. The final artifact passes 147 of 148 official tests. The coordination problem is the unrecovered verification work. 

B3. No Active Main Agent Monitoring. The main agent fails to reassess progress and act when needed. It may keep waiting even when the task is already complete or fail to use available results. In Claude Code’s RepoZero hopscotch-map test11 migration, the main agent has already built, checked, and placed the Rust port in the output directory. Near the deadline, however, it starts a ten-minute blocking wait for an unfinished sub-agent instead of finalizing the completed artifact. The execution is terminated before a final response, even though the artifact passes all 39 official tests. Unnecessary waiting prevents the main agent from closing an already completed task.

Specifically, a sub-agent may receive incomplete task requirements ( Missing Task Requirements ), separately developed components may 16 Han Li et al.

7 Benefit Analysis of Dynamic Concurrency (RQ4) 

We examine 131 pairs of concurrent and sequential execution trajectories produced by the same coding agent on the same task. Pairs are selected when concurrent execution achieves a higher test pass rate, fully solves a task that sequential execution does not, or reduces completion time by at least 60 seconds and 20% without lowering test pass rates. By comparing both trajectories, we observe three recurring situations in which concurrency brings additional benefits for agents. 

Complementary Work. One common condition that concurrency can bring improvements is that sub-agents work on different and complementary parts of the entire task in parallel while the main agent successfully integrates their results. For example, in Kimi Code’s NL2Repo justext reconstruction, eight sub-agents handle delivery files and checks while the main agent implements the core text-extraction algorithm. The main agent integrates all eight results before final verification. Though both concurrency and sequential modes pass all tests, concurrency reduces wall time from 734.116 to 467.997 seconds. Here, clearly separated responsibilities allow useful work to proceed in parallel and reduce overall completion time. 

Alternative Attempts and Selection. Dynamic concurrency can also be helpful when multiple agents develop different solutions to the same problem in parallel, allowing the main agent to select or combine the better results. In Codex’s ProgramBench Halite reconstruction, the main agent and a sub-agent independently build replacements for the black-box game environment. The main agent combines the sub-agent’s map behavior with its own stronger protocol handling, passing 292/391 tests, while the sequential mode fails to build and passes 0/391. Similarly, in NL2Repo unidecode, concurrent sub-agents provide implementations based on versions 1.3.8 and 1.4.0. After comparing them, the main agent adopts the 1.4.0 tables, passing more tests than sequential mode. In both cases, concurrency provides alternative solutions that improve the final implementation. 

Independent Validation. Dynamic concurrency is beneficial when sub-agents independently validate assumptions or implementations and provide evidence that helps the main agent correct mistakes. In Codex’s LoopsBench security labs task, a crypto sub-agent identifies the P-256 curve-order requirement for ECDSA nonce reuse while the main agent implements the broader lab suite. The main agent uses this finding to generalize the recovery algorithm, and the concurrent artifact passes all checks, whereas the sequential mode fails. Similarly, in NL2Repo trimming, a nested sub-agent’s upstream probe reveals the correct CSV header naming rules, thus the final artifact passes all tests but sequential mode cannot. In both cases, sub-agents created from concurrency provide corrective evidence early enough for the main agent to improve the final artifact. 

Finding 6: Concurrency provides concrete benefits when parallel work is effectively used by the main agent: sub-agents can complete complementary work, explore alternative solutions, or independently validate and correct the main agent’s implementation. 18 Han Li et al. 

8 Discussion 8.1 Implications 

Implications for researchers. Our taxonomy of concurrency failure patterns reveals several open research problems for building reliable concurrent coding agents. First, task orchestration 

requires better mechanisms for task decomposition, ownership assignment, and load balancing so that concurrency targets useful work without exhausting the execution budget [7]. Second, 

execution governance calls for runtime monitoring and intervention mechanisms that can detect stalled or failed sub-agents, recover unfinished work, and decide when to collect results or ter-minate execution. Third, global context management motivates better ways to communicate task requirements, interface contracts, and reusable progress across isolated agent contexts. Finally, 

shared-state coordination requires mechanisms for isolation, synchronization, and conflict detection when multiple agents concurrently modify shared files and environments [6]. Addressing these problems is important for turning dynamic concurrency from parallel agent execution into reliable collaborative software development. 

Implications for practitioners. Our findings provide practical guidance for practitioners on when to use dynamic concurrency: it should be enabled selectively rather than by default. In particular, dynamic concurrency is more promising for difficult, long-horizon tasks, whereas bounded or simpler tasks may see limited or even negative benefits. Practitioners should especially consider concurrency when a task can be clearly decomposed into independent components, supports multiple parallel attempts to the same problem, or allows independent sub-agents to validate the main implementation. At the same time, these potential benefits should be weighed against resource costs: dynamic concurrency substantially increases token usage and tool calls and often increases runtime, while performance gains remain uneven. Practitioners should therefore consider both task characteristics and available resource budgets when deciding whether and how aggressively to use dynamic concurrency [27, 28, 61]. 

8.2 Threats to Validity 

External validity. We study three native concurrency mechanisms across five benchmarks covering diverse coding tasks and difficulty levels. Our findings are therefore scoped to the tested agents and benchmarks. To address this issue, we select the frontier coding agents with their native model across a wide range of benchmarks, leading to extensive evaluation costing over $ 20K. Internal validity. Agent execution is stochastic, while the substantial cost of long-horizon evaluation limits our ability to perform large-scale repeated runs. Nevertheless, available evidence suggests that run-to-run randomness is unlikely to materially affect the overall trends observed in our study. In particular, Codex’s sequential task and test pass rates on LoopsBench in our experiments are close to the originally reported results for Codex with GPT-5.4 [30]; moreover, the original LoopsBench study reports stable overall trends across repeated attempts [30]. Moreover, manual analysis may also introduce subjective judgments. For the failure analysis, we develop a codebook through a 50-trajectory pilot and have each case independently annotated by two of three annotators, with reasoning recorded for their label assignments. Annotators discuss disagreements, and a senior author adjudicates unresolved disputes. We define explicit coding criteria and inspect complete trajectories and artifacts before identifying a failure pattern. 

9 Conclusion 

We study dynamic concurrency in three frontier coding agents through 2,124 concurrent and sequential executions across five benchmarks. Our results show that enabling concurrency increases token use while its effects on task success vary across tools and tasks. Trajectory analysis yields When Sub-Agents Work in Parallel 19 

a taxonomy of concurrency failure patterns covering task orchestration, execution governance, global context management, and shared state and merge. We also identify three mechanisms in executions with observed advantages: complementary work, alternative attempts and selection, and independent validation. Across these cases, the main agent turns concurrent activity into useful progress by integrating components, selecting candidates, or applying corrective evidence. Our findings provide an empirical basis for designing and evaluating concurrency policies that prioritize coordinated, verified delivery under finite execution budgets.
