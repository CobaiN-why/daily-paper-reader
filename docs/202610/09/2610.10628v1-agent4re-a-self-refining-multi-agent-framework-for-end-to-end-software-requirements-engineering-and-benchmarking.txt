## Abstract 

Existing LLM-based approaches for software Requirements Engi-neering (RE) typically rely on basic prompting strategies or rudi-mentary agent collaboration, under-utilizing the full potential of multi-agent systems. Meanwhile, available datasets focus on iso-lated subtasks, such as requirements extraction, classification, and completeness detection, leaving the absence of an end-to-end RE benchmark that spans from requirements elicitation to generation. We present Agent4RE – a self-refining multi-agent RE system that orchestrates specialized agents and incorporates two iterative im-provement loops. To support evaluation, we construct RE-E2E 1 – a real-world dataset built from human-written requirement specifi-cations, enabling end-to-end assessment of RE workflows. Building on this foundation, we further propose two enhanced Agent4RE versions that incorporate either autonomous self-refinement or structured human feedback, and analyze their strengths and limita-tions across different scenarios. Evaluation on 8 Large Language Models (LLMs) demonstrates that all three Agent4RE variants con-sistently outperform a domain-context-augmented prompting base-line by average 8% in text-based metrics. The two enhanced variants achieve the highest LLM-as-a-judge and human ratings, surpassing two RE baselines by approximately 0.8 points on a four-point scale. This consistent performance establishes Agent4RE as a practical end-to-end RE solution for industrial environments.

## 1 Introduction 

Requirements Engineering (RE) serves as a preliminary stage in software development, establishing a shared understanding of stake-holders’ needs, goals, and constraints while providing the founda-tion for subsequent software design, implementation, and verifica-tion [20]. Traditionally, this process has required substantial effort from multiple roles – developers, business analysts, domain experts,

and project managers – who must iteratively perform elicitation, analysis, specification, and validation together to produce complete, consistent, and clear requirement documentation [29]. Given this labor-intensive nature and the fact that defects originating from this phase are significantly more expensive to fix in later stages, improvements in RE efficiency and quality can yield substantial benefits across the entire software development lifecycle. Recently, advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) have opened new avenues for requirements engineering [11]. With supe-rior capabilities in domain knowledge inference, natural language understanding, and ambiguity detection, LLMs are well suited to accelerate and assist diverse RE sub-tasks, such as extracting re-quirements from unstructured inputs, identifying inconsistencies in RE specifications, and generating documentation based on stake-holder instructions [46]. However, existing studies predominately focus on such isolated RE sub-tasks, achieving success only on individual activities, yet offering limited support for the complex, interconnected requirements engineering processes in real-world scenarios. While recent efforts have explored LLM-based interactive systems and agents to aggregate these isolated capabilities [42], no systematic end-to-end solution has been proposed and evaluated for the full RE lifecycle. Similarly, this fragmentation extends to data resources: accessible datasets are limited to separate RE tasks and no comprehensive open-source benchmark has been released to assess the entire requirements engineering process. To address this gap, we propose Agent4RE – a self-refining multi-agent system for requirements engineering, which orchestrates specialized agents to simulate stakeholder interactions and user behaviors, enabling iterative elicitation, generation, and traceable refinement of requirements, as illustrated in Figure 1. Combined with RE-E2E – an end-to-end RE dataset, our framework mitigates the bottleneck of current LLM-based RE research, bridging isolated task-specific approaches with complete RE workflows. The main contribution of this work can be summarized as follows: (i) We propose Agent4RE – an autonomous multi-agent system for requirements engineering, which features two iterative improvement loops for self-refinement. (ii) We construct RE-E2E – an RE benchmark built from full-scale human-written requirement specifications. ## 2 Related Work 2.1 Large Language Models for RE-related Tasks 

Extensive language model-based approaches have been proposed for diverse stages throughout the requirements engineering cycle. Building on the early transformer architecture [44], researchers fine-tuned BERT to detect anaphoric ambiguity in requirements and eval-uated it against traditional supervised entity recognition techniques [9]. A similar approach was introduced to identify intra-domain ambiguities in the requirements of interdisciplinary projects [26]. Given BERT’s encoder-based architecture, parallel studies trained specialized BERT variants for requirements classification, achieving improved accuracy over machine learning techniques like decision trees and support vector machines [15, 23]. As language models have scaled up, strategic few-shot prompting has emerged as a more streamlined solution to requirements classification, deliver-ing on-par performance with fine-tuned models while eliminating the computational overhead of training [8, 40, 43]. The superior reasoning capabilities of these large-scale models enable effective requirements analysis while their generative nature aligns with the needs for requirements elicitation and specification generation. Given this potential, a preliminary evaluation [1] ex-plored the role of LLMs across different RE stages and suggested tailored prompt patterns for elicitation, analysis, specification, and validation tasks. Building on this foundation, LLMREI [19] employs LLMs as automated interviewers within a chatbot to simulate the re-quirements elicitation process with human stakeholders, designed to minimize human intervention and mitigate common interview-related errors. SpecGen [24] leveraged a two-phase prompting ap-proach to generate precise behavioral comments for program-level specifications, while ReqGPT [37], a 7B-parameter domain-specific LLM fine-tuned on over one hundred curated requirement lists, was developed to improve specification quality. Despite these break-throughs in specific RE aspects, existing methods still fall short in delivering complete requirement specifications for production envi-ronments, indicating the need for more robust and comprehensive approaches. ## 2.2 LLM-based Agentic Systems for Automated RE Process 

The collaborative workflow of multi-agent systems offers a promis-ing paradigm to tackle more complex end-to-end RE challenges [41]. As an early exploration [35], researchers implemented an LLM-based agentic system with GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 to automate user story elicitation, requirements generation, and prioritization for agile software projects. Extending this prior work, a subsequent study proposed a role-based multi-agent system that emulates stake-holder roles to collaboratively generate and prioritize requirements from project descriptions [36]. Another modular architecture ReqI-nOne breaks down requirements generation into three sub-tasks: summary, extraction, and classification [48], using tailored prompt templates to improve the quality and consistency of LLM outputs. In comparison, Elicitron has focused on more specific RE activities [2]. The system simulates elicitation interviews to explore product experiences and identify stakeholders’ needs, thus enabling the dis-covery of unforeseen use cases through structured LLM interactions. Moving toward end-to-end automation, iReDev [18], previously also called MARE [17], represents a knowledge-driven multi-agent framework designed to address the full requirements engineering process. It incorporates human expert knowledge into six agents via chain-of-thought prompting and structured reasoning, enabling them to manage an event-driven artifact pool collaboratively. Nev-ertheless, due to the absence of resources for end-to-end RE as-sessment, the evaluation focused primarily on intermediate steps such as requirements modeling and entity extraction. The final specifications were evaluated only through subjective metrics such as completeness, correctness, and consistency.

## 3 Agent4RE 

Our multi-agent system offers three different versions, each with distinct advantages: 1) a sequential workflow without feedback for streamlined RE processes; 2) a workflow with self-refinement that enables the system to iteratively improve the outputs; 3) a workflow with human feedback that incorporates domain experts to guide the refinement process.

## 4 Experiments 4.1 RE-E2E Dataset 

Existing data resources for RE predominantly focus on individual stages, suffering from several limitations: incomplete coverage, poor community visibility, and limited support for end-to-end evalua-tion [27]. For instance, PROMISE [21], a requirements classification benchmark extensively used in prior work, contains only isolated requirement items. Similarly, PURE [10] provides requirement spec-ification documents for various software systems, but lacks a unified structural template for standardized processing and systematic eval-uation. And a more recent RE dataset [4] provides a collection of user stories pairing with the corresponding domain models, yet it still excludes complete requirement specifications. As LLM capa-bilities and agentic workflows advance continuously, the ambition within the research community and industry has grown beyond ad-dressing isolated RE tasks toward autonomous management of the entire RE lifecycle. Addressing data scarcity has become an urgent priority to enable the exploration of end-to-end agentic solutions. To address this research gap, we curate RE-E2E, a dataset used to benchmark the performance of different frameworks for end-to-end software requirement engineering tasks. We selectively collect 50 complete requirement specifications in PDF format from real-world software repositories [34]. Detailed statistics of the files are presented in Table 4. All files originate from experienced software engineers and cover various software domains, such as software control, e-commerce, and security verification systems, and trans-lation software. Based on the original specification template used by developers, we divide the collected PDF files into two sets: one adhering to IEEE 830 [38], which defines the structure for traditional software requirements specifications, and another following IEEE 29148 [39], an updated standard that incorporates systems and software en-gineering requirements with more modern practices. Specifically, we use regular expressions [12] to clean noise such as unexpected newline characters and consecutive spaces, obtaining normalized plain text. However, in some edge cases, the original section names of human-written specifications do not strictly follow the IEEE format. To create a dataset with unified structure, we match these sections with the official IEEE section names based on their se-mantic embeddings. To ensure dataset reproducibility, we use the open-source Qwen3-embedding-8B to match RE-sections. If the cosine similarity between two sections falls below 0.7, human anno-tators must intervene. They review each section independently and reach consensus on whether to remove or retain it as empty. The cross-annotator agreement is 96.7%, meaning that all annotators make the same binary decision in most cases. For the remaining 3.3%, annotators follow majority vote. In Table 4, we record the section empty rates. Based on the finalized specifications, human annotators manu-ally write the initial project descriptions, which are then syntac-tically checked by LLMs to ensure grammatical correctness and formatting compliance. Notably, these annotators also serve as hu-man evaluators in experiments. Their prior experience with the annotation process ensures their familiarity with the content. Fur-ther details about their information are provided in Section 4.3. The resulting project descriptions serve as inputs for Agent4RE. The processed descriptions and software requirement specifications are provided in the supplementary materials. 

## 4.2 Selected Language Models 

Our experiments involve eight LLMs spanning different perfor-mance tiers to investigate the full potential of our agentic frame-work. We deploy four frontier models via APIs, including GPT-5 [30], GPT-4.1 [31], GPT-4o [16], and Gemini-3-flash [5], which represent the forefront of LLM capabilities. To complement these proprietary models, we incorporate four open-source alternatives that ensure reproducibility and broader applicability: LLaMA3.1-8B from Meta AI [7], Qwen3-8B from Alibaba [45], Ministral3-8B from Mistral [25], and Gemma4-4B from Google [6]. 

## 4.3 Metrics 

To compare the generated requirement specifications with the ground-truth in RE-E2E, we employ an extensive set of evaluation approaches, including text-based metrics, semantic-based metrics, LLM-as-a-judge, and human evaluators. 1) Regarding text-based metrics, we apply METEOR [3], BLEU-1 [32], and ROUGE-L [22]. METEOR evaluates text simi-larity through a harmonic mean of precision and recall, in-corporating stemming, synonymy, and word order penalties. BLEU-1 measures the proportion of individual words (uni-grams) in the generated text that match the reference text, while ROUGE-L evaluates the longest common subsequence between the candidate and reference texts. (2) For semantic-based metrics, we apply BERTScore [47] and 

SentenceBert [33]. BERTScore computes token-level seman-tic similarity by comparing BERT-based word embeddings between the candidate and reference texts, while Sentence-Bert measures sentence-level similarity by encoding texts into dense vector representations and calculating their co-sine similarity. (3) LLM-as-a-judge leverages prompt-based assessment to grade the generated requirement specifications. Figure 4 presents the prompt template used for this approach, with grades assigned on a scale from 1 to 4. To assess RE quality in practice, developers typically require a language checker for ambiguity, an app-lifecycle tool for completeness, and a formal/model checker for logical consistency, leading to increased toolchain complexity. Since these quality criteria are fundamentally grounded in written text and require-ments, LLMs are natural requirements evaluators given their expertise in language processing. However, this suitability does not apply to all model scales. While large-scale models demonstrate promising capabilities in judging text quality, compact models often struggle [14] – even the best one misjudges. Therefore, we deploy this evaluation strategy ex-clusively to four top-tier models (GPT-5, GPT-4.1, GPT-4o, and Gemini-3-flash). (4) We further include human evaluation for comparison. To align with LLM-as-a-judge, three software developers from industry assess summaries independently on a scale from 1 to 4, with final scores averaged for fairness. ## 4.4 RE Specification Generation 

Since iterative human feedback has no theoretical upper limit on performance, we perform one optimization loop for all Agent4RE variants to ensure fair comparison. Table 5 demonstrates the eval-uation results for end-to-end specification generation in the next page. Agent4RE significantly outperforms the two baselines in most considered metrics, with each variant exhibiting superior perfor-mance in different evaluation dimensions. For traditional text- and semantic-based metrics, incorporating high-level feedback from human developers yields the best results, achieving 0.251 in ME-TEOR, 0.273 in Rouge-L, 0.063 in BLEU-1, 0.838 in BERT F1 score, and 0.392 in Sentence-BERT similarity. As for LLM-as-a-judge, pure self-refinement with GPT-5 slightly outperforms the human-in-the-loop variant, with both GPT-5 and GPT-4.1 models securing perfect 4.0 ratings when evaluated by GPT-4.1. However, upon human eval-uation, self-refinement with human feedback still performs best, scoring the highest 3.29 out of 4. In comparison, the two baselines fall short, reaching 2.22 and 2.87 respectively in human evaluation. If we zoom in model performance for detailed analysis, three GPT models lead the performance in most metrics, especially when deployed within the sequential workflow without feedback or self-refinement with human feedback. Among them, the latest GPT-5 model demonstrates exceptional performance in LLM-as-a-judge and human evaluation, achieving near-perfect scores from other LLM evaluators when integrated into the Agent4RE framework. However, an unexpected finding emerges with pure self-refinement: iterative improvement loops lead to degraded performance on semantic-based metrics, as evidenced by Sentence BERT. This phe-nomenon may stem from LLMs’ deliberate attempts to modify content, and such purposeful revisions can result in substantial semantic drift. We provide a case study in the supplementary mate-rials, including an example of software project description, human feedback, and the generated requirement specifications. Due to the length of the generated content, a separate JSON file recording the output of each agent throughout the complete agentic workflow is included in the uploaded repository. Detailed resource consumption for each model is reported in Table 6. Among all models, GPT-5 leads to the highest cost, consum-ing 33.74K tokens per specification at an estimated cost of 0.127$ and producing 4.68K tokens on average in the outputs. With respect to inference latency, it takes approximately 33.7 seconds to com-plete the full agentic workflow and generate the final requirement specification. In comparison, other large models are more efficient, generally requiring fewer tokens and inference time per specifica-tion, but this result is a trade-off in performance and output quality, as evidenced by LLM-as-a-judge and human evaluation – two main metrics in Table 5. We run the four compact models locally through Ollama, thus introducing no API cost. But given our hardware – MAC pro with an Apple M3 chip and 36GB of memory – inference latency remains considerable, particularly for Qwen3-8B, which requires 66.5 seconds per specification. ## 4.5 Comparison among Agentic Approaches 

Following the discussion of model performance on RE, we compare the baselines and all Agent4RE variants by averaging the perfor-mance across all LLMs. For conciseness, we present the results using four representative metrics from each category: human evaluation, GPT-5-as-a-judge, BERTScore, and METEOR. As illustrated in Figure 5, all three Agent4RE variants outperform the two baselines. Self-refinement with human feedback achieves the best average score across all four metrics. Compared to the prompting baseline, this variant achieves approximately 4% im-provements in METEOR and an average of 0.8 points in human and LLM evaluation. However, we notice a dip in performance in the lower bound of pure self-refinement, particularly in METEOR and BERTScore, which indicates that less capable language models struggle to support iterative self-improvement, and a minimum threshold of model capacity is required to make self-refinement strategies effective. Furthermore, an upward trend is observed from left to right, reflecting the progressive advancements in three key dimensions: from basic prompting strategies to more complicated agentic approaches, from linear workflows to iterative self-refined loops, and from model-only generation to human-guided collabo-rative systems.

## 5 Conclusions and Future Work 

The proposed Agent4RE framework significantly outperforms exist-ing baselines on the newly released RE-E2E dataset, leveraging five collaborative self-refining agents. While the pure self-refinement variant strengthens the basic sequential workflow, it requires suf-ficient model capacity to be effective. After multiple iterative im-provement loops, less capable models may struggle and produce content with substantial semantic drift. In comparison, strategically incorporating human expertise at critical decision point proves to be beneficial for the agentic system, guiding them toward desired quality and generating consistent RE specifications. Furthermore, we analyze the impact of self-refinement loops for each LLM. The results reveal that model stability, i.e. lower variance across iter-ations, serves as a stronger performance indicator than the total number of executed iterations. This work opens several promising avenues for future research. Building on Agent4RE, we encourage exploring alternative self-refining architectures, incorporating specialized agents to optimize the performance. Moreover, as LLMs continue to evolve, bench-marking new models within this agentic framework will provide valuable insights into how model capability enhancement trans-lates to improved specification generation performance. Beyond RE-E2E, the research community also requires more end-to-end benchmarks that involve structured RE specification annotations. While our dataset uses well-formed project descriptions to establish a controlled baseline, real-world briefs could be ambiguous. To bet-ter reflect such settings, future work could introduce a stress-test subset with ambiguous briefs to assess their impact on evaluation quality.
