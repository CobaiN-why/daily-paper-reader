Davis Abstract Software performance optimization requires identifying bottlenecks and improvement opportunities, and empirically evaluating the effects and trade-offs across performance objectives. Autonomous AI coding agents sub-mit performance-oriented pull requests (PRs) to open-source projects, yet it remains unclear whether these changes reflect established optimization prac-tices and adequately substantiate their claimed value. Extending our MSR 2026 Mining Challenge pilot study of 407 performance PRs, we analyze a sample of 1,130 agentic and 1,130 human-authored perfor-mance PRs collected through June 2026. We compare adoption outcomes and patch characteristics between the two groups, and examine their optimiza-tion practices and validation behaviors. The results show that agentic PRs are merged less often than human-authored PRs (54.4% vs. 73.3%), but the two groups use similar optimization strategies and report validation at compara-ble rates. Agentic PRs rely more on static reasoning and less on benchmarks (46.1% vs. 57.2%), although benchmark use converges by the end of the study period. Agentic PRs also report validation for code-smell refactorings as often as for resource-targeting changes, whereas human-authored PRs do so less of-ten. 1 Introduction 

Software performance directly affects the reliability (Ulrich, 2016), user ex-perience (Liu and Heer, 2014), and energy efficiency (Pereira et al., 2017) of modern systems. Despite sustained advances in compiler optimization (Much-nick, 1997), algorithm design (Cormen et al., 2022), and hardware-aware tun-ing (Bryant and O’Hallaron, 2016), performance engineering remains labor-intensive and requires specialized expertise and systematic measurement (Gregg, 2020). The difficulty arises in part because an optimization can have non-local effects, depend on assumptions about workloads and hardware, and require empirical evidence, such as benchmarking or profiling, to establish its im-pact (Mytkowicz et al., 2009). Large language models (LLMs) increasingly support software development tasks such as code synthesis, debugging, and refactoring (Hou et al., 2024), and have shown potential to improve non-functional properties such as execu-tion time and energy efficiency (Shypula et al., 2024; Peng et al., 2026a). More recently, autonomous coding agents have begun authoring substantial num-bers of pull requests (PRs) to open-source projects (Watanabe et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025), including changes intended to improve performance. However, empirical evidence about how agents optimize software in real-world projects remains limited. Assessing agentic performance PRs requires examining what changes they make and what evidence supports their claimed benefits. A plau-sible optimization may lack measurements under representative workloads or overlook costs in other resource dimensions. Examining the changes alongside their supporting evidence helps characterize agentic performance work and supports maintainers in evaluating agent-authored PRs. This article extends our MSR 2026 Mining Challenge paper (Peng et al., 2026b), which reported a pilot study of 407 performance-related PRs (324 agentic and 83 human-authored) drawn from AIDev (Li et al., 2025). The pilot established the study’s initial empirical framework: it adapted the SysLLMatic optimization catalog (Peng et al., 2026a) to real-world PRs, expanding it from 43 to 59 patterns, and developed a taxonomy for classifying reported validation evidence. It also provided initial evidence that agentic and human-authored PRs used similar optimization strategies but differed in how frequently they reported validation. The pilot comparison was limited, however, by its small, How Do Coding Agents Optimize Software and Report Performance Validation? 3

imbalanced sample and by different repository-inclusion thresholds for agentic and human-authored PRs ( ≥100 versus ≥500 stars). This work uses the pilot study as its methodological foundation and extends the investigation. We reconstruct the AIDev collection pipeline, extending its coverage through June 2026—ten months beyond AIDev-v3—and apply the same repository criteria to both groups, producing a sample of 1,130 agentic and 1,130 human-authored performance PRs. We retain the pilot’s methodol-ogy for analyzing optimization patterns and validation practices as RQ2 and RQ3, formalize and expand its methodology for analyzing adoption and main-tainability as RQ1, and introduce RQ4 to examine the quantitative metrics reported as validation evidence. We ask: 

– RQ1: How do agentic and human-authored performance PRs differ in adop-tion outcomes, patch size, and structural indicators of maintainability? 

– RQ2: Which optimization patterns occur in agentic and human-authored performance PRs, and how does their prevalence differ between the two groups? 

– RQ3: How do agentic and human-authored performance PRs differ in their reported testing and validation practices, and how do these practices change over time? 

– RQ4: What quantitative performance metrics, if any, are reported in the validation evidence of agentic and human-authored performance PRs, and how do those reported metrics relate to the applied optimization? Our results suggest that agents have largely converged with humans in the optimizations they propose, but not in how they substantiate their effective-ness. Agentic and human-authored PRs draw on much the same optimiza-tion strategies and report validation at comparable rates (82.3% vs. 80.6%), yet agentic PRs are merged less often (54.4% vs. 73.3%) and lean more on static reasoning than on benchmarks (46.1% vs. 57.2%). Comparable rates also mask a difference in judgment: human-authored PRs report validation evidence for code-smell refactorings less often than for changes that target a performance resource (73.5% vs. 82.8%), whereas agentic PRs show no such distinction (83.0% vs. 81.7%). More importantly, neither group routinely mea-sures whether a change improves performance or what it costs: among PRs with validation evidence, only 50.9% of agentic PRs and 52.4% of human-authored PRs report a performance metric at all. An important open problem for agent-authored optimization is therefore producing measurements that es-tablish the intended effect and expose its trade-offs. 

Significance. 2 Background & Related Work 

This section provides the background for the four research questions and posi-tions our study relative to prior work. Section 2.1 introduces the performance-engineering concepts underlying our analysis: the optimization-pattern cata-log used to characterize performance PRs (RQ2), forms of validation evidence (RQ3), and performance metrics (RQ4). Section 2.2 discusses the transition from AI-assisted to autonomous software engineering and LLM-based code optimization. Section 2.3 reviews related work on empirical studies of AI-generated performance changes. 2.1 Performance Engineering 

2.1.1 Optimization Patterns 

Performance engineering spans the full software stack, from application logic to operating system and hardware behavior. At the source-code level, perfor-mance tuning includes, for example, replacing an inefficient algorithm or data structure, caching repeated computation, improving memory locality, simpli-fying control flow, and parallelizing independent work. These techniques are extensively studied across the algorithm, compiler, computer-architecture, and systems-performance literature (Cormen et al., 2022; Muchnick, 1997; Bryant and O’Hallaron, 2016; Gregg, 2020), but the literature describes them at dif-ferent levels of abstraction and for different purposes. To answer RQ2, we require a concrete and consistently defined vocabulary for labeling the optimization strategies used in performance-related PRs. We adopt the SysLLMatic catalog (Peng et al., 2026a) because it consolidates 43 source-level optimization patterns into seven categories and demonstrates their use in guiding whole-program optimization across real-world benchmarks. Its organization at both category and pattern levels provides the granularity needed to compare broad strategy distributions while retaining the specific mechanism applied in each PR. 

2.1.2 Performance Validation 

Optimizations require rigorous validation because performance is highly sen-sitive to measurement noise, workload choice, and experimental design; best practices therefore emphasize representative workloads, repeatable protocols, and statistically grounded comparisons (Hoefler and Belli, 2015). In software engineering practice, this evidence can take several forms. First, Benchmark-Based Validation relies on quantitative measurements ( e.g., unit tests or mi-crobenchmarks) to directly assess performance impact (Horký et al., 2015; Hoefler and Belli, 2015). Second, Profiling-Based Validation uses profiling ar-tifacts such as hotspot traces, CPU samples, or flame graphs to localize perfor-mance costs (Gregg, 2016). Third, Static-Reasoning-Based Validation supports How Do Coding Agents Optimize Software and Report Performance Validation? 5

performance claims through algorithmic arguments without relying on runtime data, e.g., by identifying asymptotic inefficiencies (Olivo et al., 2015). We use these three forms of validation evidence as the basis for answering RQ3, which examines how agentic and human-authored performance PRs report support for their performance claims. 

2.1.3 Performance Metrics and Trade-offs 

Performance metrics characterize different dimensions of a system’s behav-ior. The systems-performance literature commonly considers measures such as latency and execution time, throughput, memory footprint and alloca-tion pressure, CPU work, I/O and network traffic, concurrency behavior, and cost (Gregg, 2020). Other dimensions include artifact size, such as compiled code size (Cummins et al., 2025); build time, including compilation time (Jay-atilaka et al., 2021); and energy consumption (Pereira et al., 2017). Impor-tantly, optimizations can affect these dimensions differently, either improv-ing several metrics simultaneously or introducing trade-offs between them. For example, eliminating redundant computation, dead code, or duplicated queries can reduce execution time and resource use simultaneously (Muchnick, 1997; Sellis, 1988). In contrast, caching and memoization can reduce execution time while increasing memory use (Michie, 1968; Gregg, 2020); parallelization can reduce wall-clock time while increasing CPU and memory use (Cormen et al., 2022); and compression can reduce bandwidth and storage requirements while increasing CPU work (Abadi et al., 2006). 2.2.1 From AI Assistance to Autonomous Software Engineering 

Early applications of code-trained large language models (LLMs) to software engineering primarily supported developer-directed tasks, such as code com-pletion and local code synthesis (Chen et al., 2021; Nijkamp et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2026). Their use has since expanded across the software development life-cycle, including code generation, program repair, testing, and refactoring (Hou et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024a). In these AI-assisted settings, developers are responsible for integrating and evaluating generated changes. More recent systems incorporate LLMs into autonomous coding agents that perform multi-step software-engineering tasks, including repository ex-ploration, multi-file editing, tool invocation, and iterative revision based on tool feedback (Yang et al., 2024; Shinn et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025). 2.2.2 LLM-Based Code Optimization 

A growing body of work uses LLMs to improve the performance of exist-ing code (Gong et al., 2025). Some approaches learn optimization strategies from human-written code changes. PIE fine-tunes models on pairs of slow and How Do Coding Agents Optimize Software and Report Performance Validation? 7

fast competitive-programming submissions (Shypula et al., 2024), while Su-personic trains a sequence-to-sequence model on pairs of functionally equiva-lent C/C++ competitive-programming submissions to generate targeted, diff-based source-level optimizations (Chen et al., 2024). Other approaches guide LLMs at inference time using execution feedback or iterative self-optimization, as in PerfCodeGen (Peng et al., 2025a) and EffiLearner (Huang et al., 2024). Related benchmarks, including EvalPerf and COFFE, evaluate the efficiency of code generated by LLMs (Liu et al., 2024; Peng et al., 2025b). LLMs have also been applied to lower layers of the software stack, including compiler optimiza-tion passes (Cummins et al., 2025) and parallel and HPC programs through agentic systems (Wei et al., 2024). Energy has also been studied alongside execution time: LLMs can generate more energy-efficient code (Peng et al., 2024), while subsequent work benchmarks the energy consumption of gener-ated code (Islam et al., 2025) and finds that runtime reductions can sometimes come with increased energy use (Coignion et al., 2025). SysLLMatic (Peng et al., 2026a) extends this work to whole-program optimization by combining performance diagnostics and iterative refinement across languages, improv-ing latency, throughput, energy, and memory. Together, this body of work establishes that LLMs can generate effective code optimizations. Our study complements these efforts by examining how autonomous coding agents apply optimization strategies in real-world pull requests and how the accompanying validation evidence compares with that reported by human contributors. 2.3 Related Work Prior works have mined real-world patches to study performance optimization. RAPGen (Garg et al., 2025) mines performance-related GitHub commits to build a transformation knowledge base for fixing code inefficiencies, demon-strating that historical patches encode reusable optimization actions. Yi et al. (Yi et al., 2026) uses performance-improving commits with JMH bench-marks as ground truth, asks an LLM to optimize the pre-change code, and compares LLM-generated optimizations against both original and developer-authored improvements. Concurrently, recent work has begun empirically ex-amining agentic PRs in real-world repositories, comparing their characteristics and integration outcomes with human-authored PRs (Watanabe et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025). Prior to our pilot study, no work had compared agentic and human-authored performance pull requests or analyzed performance factors such as optimization strategies and validation practices. The pilot (Peng et al., 2026b) provided the first such comparison across 407 PRs and developed the optimization catalog and validation taxonomy used in this article. 3.1.1 Pilot-Study Dataset 

The pilot study was conducted using AIDev (Li et al., 2025), a dataset of GitHub pull requests (PRs) authored by agents and humans with commit, diff, review, and repository-level metadata. It used two subsets constructed un-der different repository-popularity thresholds: AIDev-Pop , containing 33,596 PRs from repositories with at least 100 stars, and Human-PR , containing 6,618 PRs from repositories with at least 500 stars. Restricting these subsets to PRs labeled perf yielded 340 agentic PRs and 88 human-authored PRs. To support code-level analysis, the study retrieved fine-grained commit diffs from GitHub. It reconstructed 7,874 commit-level records for the 88 human-authored PRs; when combined with AIDev-Pop , the dataset con-tained 15,284 records from 428 performance PRs. Quality filtering removed records with missing filenames (46), configuration-only changes (44), merge commits (10,166), and commits from deleted repositories (74). The resulting dataset comprised 4,954 valid records from 407 PRs and retained coverage of 324/340 agentic PRs (95.3%) and 83/88 human-authored PRs (94.3%). 

Limitations of this dataset: The initial dataset had three limitations for di-rectly comparing agentic and human-authored performance PRs. First, the two groups were substantially imbalanced, with 324 agentic PRs and 83 human-authored PRs after filtering. Second, they were drawn using different repository-popularity thresholds, with agentic PRs collected from repositories with at least 100 stars and human-authored PRs from repositories with at least 500 stars. Third, the collection ended in August 2025 and therefore did not rep-resent subsequent changes in agent activity and development practices. These limitations motivate the reconstructed dataset described next. 

3.1.2 Large-Scale Extension and Balanced Sample 

For the large-scale extension, we reconstruct and extend the AIDev dataset to address the limitations identified above. Specifically, we extend the observa-tion period, draw agentic and human-authored PRs from the same repository population, and balance the two groups by submission week. Accuracy of task-type classification: Because our performance PR classifier uses a different LLM from AIDev, we first assess the agreement between the task-type labels produced by the two classification pipelines for PRs shared by both collections. This comparison does not treat either set of labels as ground truth. Using AIDev’s labels as the reference for this pairwise comparison, our classifier obtains 64.4% precision, 72.5% recall, and an F1 score of 68.2%. We separately construct a manually annotated ground-truth set to evaluate performance PR classification accuracy. We randomly sample 100 PRs from the mined dataset, and one author manually labels each PR as performance-related or non-performance-related based on its content. Against these anno-tations, our classifier achieves 83.3% precision, 84.7% recall, and an F1 score of 84.0%. To compare our classifier with the original AIDev classifier against the same ground truth, we further restrict the evaluation to the 28 annotated PRs that appear in both datasets. On this shared subset, our classifier achieves higher recall (82.4% vs. 52.9%) and F1 score (80.0% vs. 64.3%), while AIDev achieves higher precision (81.8% vs. 77.8%).

3.3.2 Labeling Procedure 

We use LLMs to assign optimization labels and use iterative human inspection to calibrate the annotation procedure (Wang et al., 2024b). 

Pilot study: The pilot study used two commercial LLMs, GPT-5.1 and Gemini-3-Pro-Preview , with temperature set to zero, to classify each patch inde-pendently. Labels on which the models agreed were accepted directly, while disagreements were reviewed and adjudicated by two human annotators (Peng et al., 2026b; Ahmed et al., 2025). This procedure had two limitations for the large-scale extension. First, manually adjudicating every model disagreement does not scale readily to the substantially larger dataset analyzed here. Second, agreement between the two models did not guarantee a correct label: a manual audit of a 10% random sample of model-agreed cases in the pilot study estimated an error rate of 10% (Peng et al., 2026b). These limitations motivate the revised consensus procedure used in the large-scale extension. 

Large-scale extension: We obtain independent labels from three models and retain a label only when at least two models agree on the category-pattern pair, without subsequent human adjudication. GPT-5.6-sol , Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview , and Qwen3.8-27B each assign one category and one pattern to every PR in the sample of 2,260, with temperature set to zero. This proce-dure requires corroboration by a second model and applies a uniform decision rule to ambiguous cases while remaining feasible at the scale of the dataset. 3.4.1 Validation Evidence Taxonomy 

The large-scale extension retains the pilot study’s validation-evidence taxon-omy unchanged, preserving a consistent measure across both stages. Because PR artifacts do not record the development process itself, we analyze the val-idation described in those artifacts, rather than directly observing how it was performed. 9 We treat explicit claims of testing or evaluation as evidence that these activities occurred, while acknowledging that a reported claim may be incomplete or inaccurate, particularly when generated by a model. Following the taxonomy developed in the pilot study, we classify reported validation evidence as Benchmark-Based Validation , Profiling-Based Valida-tion , Static-Reasoning-Based Validation , or Anecdotal or Informal Local Test-ing . The first three categories are defined in Section 2.1.2; the fourth captures validation claims without quantitative measurements or analytical support. PRs containing no evidence are labeled as having no performance validation.

3.4.3 Aggregate and Temporal Comparisons 

We compare the two author types in terms of both validation presence and the distribution of primary validation types. Both are categorical outcomes, so we use chi-square tests to determine whether they differ by author type and report Cramér’s V to quantify the size of the association. Aggregate comparisons across the 65 sampled ISO weeks may obscure changes over time. We therefore also examine whether two outcomes change during the observation period: whether validation is reported and, among PRs 20 H. Peng et al. 

with a resolved evidence type, whether benchmark-based validation is the pri-mary evidence. The 1:1 balance within each ISO week permits the groups to be compared over the same calendar periods. Both temporal outcomes are binary, so we use logistic regression to estimate how they change over time and whether that change differs between agentic and human-authored PRs. For each temporal outcome Yi, we fit the following model: 

logit Pr( Yi = 1) = β0 + β1Ai + β2Ti + β3AiTi, (1) where Ai indicates an agentic PR and Ti denotes the PR’s creation time in years, centered on the sample mean. The interaction term β3 captures whether the temporal trend differs between agentic and human-authored PRs. We test this difference using a Wald test and cluster the standard errors by repository to account for PRs from the same repository not being fully independent. 3.5.2 Reported Metrics and Optimization Correspondence 

We first characterize metric reporting separately by author type. For each PR, we count the number of distinct metrics reported; across PRs, we calculate the frequency of each metric. We then assess whether the reported metrics corre-spond to the expected benefits of the applied optimization. For each catalog pattern, we map the catalog’s optimized metrics (Peng et al., 2026a) to the taxonomy in Table 1 and determine whether a PR reports at least one metric in the resulting set. To determine whether the observed correspondence exceeds what would be expected by chance, we use a one-sided, 20,000-iteration per-mutation test. The permutation procedure reassigns the expected-metric sets among optimization patterns while holding each PR’s reported metrics fixed, thereby preserving the observed metric-reporting distribution while breaking the mapping between optimization patterns and their expected metrics. We use a one-sided test because the hypothesis of interest is whether correspon-dence is greater than the permutation baseline. Finally, we examine how metric reporting varies by optimization category and validation-evidence type, separately for agentic and human-authored PRs. For each RQ2 category, we construct a metric profile comprising the frequency of every reported metric. We also compare the proportion of PRs reporting at least one metric in benchmark-based evidence with the corresponding pro-portion in each other RQ3 evidence type. 5 Threats to Validity 

We discuss construct, internal, conclusion, and external threats to validity (Wohlin et al., 2012). 

Construct Validity: Merge outcomes reflect repository integration workflows and do not directly measure review effort or change quality. NLOC, average cyclomatic complexity, and function count capture code size and control-flow structure, but not readability, duplication, or architectural impact. Dataset inclusion partly relies on an automated classifier evaluated against 100 PRs labeled by one author; classification and reference-label errors may therefore affect the sample. The accepted RQ2 and RQ3 consensus labels were not independently re-audited and may contain classification errors. RQ3 also measures documented rather than directly observed validation and may omit unreported activities. Conversely, a reported benchmark does not establish that it was executed as described or that its results were reported accurately. Agentic systems may also learn to reproduce the form of human validation reports, including plausible benchmark claims, without a corresponding im-provement in measurement practice or evidential reliability; our artifact-based analysis cannot distinguish such imitation from genuine execution. RQ4 neither verifies performance effects nor reproduces experiments. The metric extractor’s audit estimates 87.5% precision but not recall, so reported frequencies may include false positives and omit evidence in figures, screen-shots, or linked artifacts. 

Internal Validity: The observational design identifies associations rather than causal effects of agentic authorship. Weekly stratification balances submission time but not repository, programming language, contributor population, or in-tegration workflow, any of which may contribute to the observed associations. Exclusions for unavailable code metrics or unresolved labels could also intro-duce bias if omitted PRs differ systematically from retained PRs, although retention rates are similar across author types. 

Conclusion Validity: Repository dependence is addressed only by the clus-tered standard errors in the RQ3 temporal analysis; the remaining inferences 36 H. Peng et al. 

are unadjusted. The RQ4 correspondence result depends on the randomization assumptions of its permutation procedure. We thus interpret statistical signif-icance along with effect sizes and the assumptions underlying each analysis. 

External Validity: Our findings are limited to performance PRs in AIDev’s public GitHub repository frame, which applies a 100-star threshold, and to its five covered agents through June 1, 2026. They may not generalize to less prominent or proprietary repositories, other agents, later agent versions, or settings without visible agent involvement. Because the agentic group contains autonomously authored and submitted PRs, it does not represent human-controlled AI-assisted development. 

6 Discussion 

6.1 Agents as a Path to Stronger Performance Validation Performance optimization has historically been a difficult software engineer-ing task, in part because establishing whether a change actually improves performance requires careful measurement, appropriate workloads, and con-trol of confounding factors. Our results show that these challenges persist in human-authored PRs: although validation is common, quantitative measure-ments and trade-off analysis remain sparse across both groups (Sections 4.3 and 4.4). This finding suggests that matching current human practice should not be the ultimate goal for coding agents. As agents take on a larger role in performance-critical development, they could raise the standard of practice by making rigorous measurement, trade-off analysis, and reproducible validation routine parts of the optimization process. Agentic systems should therefore be evaluated not only on whether they match current developer practices, but also on whether they strengthen them. 6.2 Towards Next-Gen Code Optimization Our analysis shows that agentic PRs draw from a similar optimization-pattern space as human-authored PRs (Section 4.2), highlighting both the effective-ness of current LLMs in acquiring established performance knowledge and the limits of their practical behavior. This observed similarity may reflect that agents are genuinely capable of applying the same complex optimizations as humans, or it may indicate that agents have learned from human-written arti-facts what opportunities to recognize and thus reproduce human blind spots. Our data do not directly measure the available optimization opportunities, the result therefore demonstrates convergence in observed strategy, not equiv-alence in underlying optimization capability. Distinguishing these possibilities will require comparisons on matched bottlenecks that vary the available opti-mization opportunities. Looking within this shared strategy space reveals a further opportunity: loop transformations remain the least applied category (Fig. 6), despite decades of work showing the effectiveness of techniques such as unrolling (Sarkar, 2000) and fusion (Manjikian and Abdelrahman, 1997). In human development work-flows, loop techniques are often avoided because they can reduce code readabil-ity and maintainability and are error-prone to implement correctly (McKinley et al., 1996; Mens and Tourwé, 2004). These constraints may affect agentic workflows differently. As software development becomes increasingly agent-centric, this gap presents an opportunity to investigate whether agents can apply optimization techniques that are traditionally underutilized by humans. 6.3 From Presence of Validation to Completeness of Evidence Estimating performance impact is challenging, as sensitivity to workloads, hardware, and measurement noise often yields misleading or irreproducible results (Mytkowicz et al., 2009; Papadopoulos et al., 2021). Thus, rigorous improvements require validation through benchmarking or profiling (Beyer et al., 2019; Hoefler and Belli, 2015). Our results show both progress and gaps in agentic validation practice. Across the full sample, agentic PRs rely more often on static reasoning and human-authored PRs more often report benchmarks (Fig. 7), but benchmark use among agentic PRs increases over time and reaches the human-authored rate by the end of the study period (Section 4.3.2). This convergence is encouraging, yet using benchmarks does not establish that agents select evidence appropriate to a particular change. In addition, agents may have learned to imitate the conventions of human benchmark reporting, including emitting plausible-looking results, and our data cannot distinguish improved measurement from improved presentation. We also observe that human-authored PRs report validation evidence and quantitative metrics for code-smell refactorings less often than for resource-targeting performance changes, a pattern consistent with evidence reporting varying with the nature of the change; agentic PRs show no statistically sig-nificant corresponding difference (Section 4.4.2). In addition, a gap appears in trade-off reporting: caching and buffering PRs in both groups rarely document an adverse memory effect alongside a reported runtime improvement. The as-surance target should therefore be the appropriateness and completeness of evidence, not merely the presence of validation or a benchmark (Section 4.4). Performance spans several metrics, and optimizations often exchange one re-source for another (Section 2.1.3); even benchmark-based evidence can verify an intended gain while leaving relevant costs unobserved. As the volume of agent-authored PRs grows, evaluation infrastructure should connect the opti-mization being attempted to representative workloads, expected metrics, and likely trade-offs. Existing CI-based benchmarking, designed for human work-flows, is unlikely to scale to agent-driven workloads, motivating standardized benchmarks and shared profiling platforms for automated validation. 38 H. Peng et al. 

6.4 Trustworthiness under Increasing Autonomy As AI-assisted software engineering becomes more autonomous, human respon-sibility shifts toward specifying goals, constraints, and criteria for accepting a change. Model-Based Agentic Software Engineering (MAGE) frames this challenge in terms of Modeling , which makes consequential knowledge and in-tent explicit, and Alignment , which gives established obligations operational force (Davis et al., 2026). For code optimization, a model could specify the target metric, workload, baseline, measurement protocol, and acceptable costs in other resources. Benchmark results can then be assessed against a stated objective rather than treated as sufficient evidence on their own. Alignment would enforce the established parts of that model through bench-mark and profiling measurements, statistical and resource checks, constraints, and acceptance gates. These checks matter because performance cannot be inferred from code alone: a functionally correct change may yield no improve-ment, regress on other workloads, or appear beneficial because of measurement noise or experimental conditions (Mytkowicz et al., 2009; Hoefler and Belli, 2015; Jain, 1991; Beyer et al., 2019). Passing tests also cannot rule out other defects in LLM- and agent-authored patches (Xia et al., 2023; Peng et al., 2026c; Sajadi et al., 2025). In our sample, reported validation becomes more common over time (Section 4.3.2), but quantitative evidence and trade-off reporting remain limited (Section 4.4), and PR descriptions provide only au-thors’ accounts of what was measured. Although our artifacts do not reveal the projects’ governed engineering environments, the results show why validation cannot be merely another agent activity: trustworthy acceptance must join an explicit performance model, independent evidence, and enforceable obli-gations, while retaining human authority over unresolved trade-offs and the sufficiency of that evidence. 6.5 Implications for Longitudinal Agentic SE Datasets The temporal analysis in RQ3 (Section 4.3.2) shows that agent validation prac-tices can change substantially within a single collection window. An Agentic SE dataset therefore captures a time-bound state of practice rather than a sta-ble characterization of agent behavior. Conclusions about differences between agentic and human-authored PRs may consequently depend on the observation period, even when the same analysis is applied. Reproducible longitudinal comparisons also require transparent author-ship attribution. Our conservative filtering (Section 3.1.2) shows that the human-authored group is defined by the absence of selected automation and AI-assistance signals, rather than by verified human authorship. Dataset re-leases should therefore preserve the raw attribution signals, record when they were collected, and version the classification rules applied to them. Where possible, they should distinguish agentic, observably AI-assisted, and human-authored PRs. Combined with time-balanced sampling or period-specific re-How Do Coding Agents Optimize Software and Report Performance Validation? 39 

porting, these practices would help future studies distinguish changes in devel-opment behavior from changes in attribution coverage or classification rules. 

7 Conclusion 

We extend our pilot study of 407 performance PRs to a larger sample of 2,260 agentic and human-authored PRs collected through June 2026. Using a 59-pattern catalog and evidence reported in PR artifacts, we compare adoption and patch characteristics and examine optimization strategies, validation prac-tices, and reported performance metrics. Our results show that agentic and human-authored PRs use broadly similar optimization strategies and report validation at comparable rates, but agentic PRs rely more on static reasoning and human-authored PRs more on benchmarks; agentic benchmark use in-creases over time. Across both groups, only about half of PRs with validation evidence report an extractable performance metric, and relevant trade-offs are rarely documented. These findings shift the central challenge from generating plausible op-timizations to demonstrating that they work. As agents assume more au-tonomous roles, performance changes should be accepted only when the ev-idence measures the intended effect under representative conditions and ac-counts for relevant resource trade-offs. Evaluation pipelines must therefore make quantitative, multidimensional validation a requirement rather than treating any validation statement as sufficient. More broadly, trustworthy agentic software engineering depends not only on what agents can produce, but also on engineering systems that make their objectives explicit, their evidence independently verifiable, and their acceptance conditions enforceable.
