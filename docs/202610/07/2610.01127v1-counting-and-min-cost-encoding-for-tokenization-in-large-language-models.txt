# Abstract 

Mainstream large language models (LLMs) rely on a tokenizer to encode text into a token sequence. Different tokenizers may yield token sequences of substantially different lengths for the same text. With a fixed model architecture, shorter token sequences correspond to lower inference time. We propose a tokenizer training approach named Counting and Filtering (CNF) and a text encoding algorithm called Min-Cost Encoding (MCE). MCE defines a cost function over a text segment, and determines the best segmentation by globally minimizing the overall segmentation cost. CNF builds a raw vocabulary by directly counting valid substrings, and then constructs the final vocabulary through a filtering step based on actual token usage when segmenting the training corpus with MCE. The CNF-MCE conbination offers several advantages over BPE (the most widely used tokenizer algorithm for LLMs so far), including higher token effi-ciency, greater scalability, and lower dependency. Across six text categories and two vocabulary-size groups, CNF-MCE consistently achieves better compression than the evaluated BPE tokenizers. With a 250K vocabulary, CNF-MCE increases compression rate by 26% and 30% on English web text over the o200k base and qwen250k tokenizers. Experiments scaling the vocabulary to 1M entries on English web text demonstrate sustained improvements over BPE, with a token efficiency improvement of over 60% and vocabulary utilization rising from 52.9% to 96.9%. The MCE algorithm does not depend on a merge list (as in BPE) or token probability (as in Uni-gramLM), making it applicable to a wide range of vocabularies, including those built from BPE, UnigramLM, CNF, and others. Language models trained from scratch at the 1.8B and 8B scales achieve comparable average performance to models using the BPE tokenizers across 11 benchmarks. These results demonstrate that CNF-MCE can improve token efficiency significantly while maintaining competitive downstream performance. 

# 1 Introduction 

Transformer-based large language models (LLMs) cannot process raw text directly. They rely on a tokenizer to convert input text to a sequence of tokens, a process referred to as text encoding, tokenization, or segmentation. Similarly, the output token IDs produced by LLMs are converted back into text through a decoding process. A tokenizer is primarily defined by a vocabulary, an encoding algorithm, and a decoding algorithm. The choice of tokenizers directly affects both the efficiency and performance of LLMs. For the same text, different tokenizers may produce token sequences of substantially different lengths. With a fixed model architecture, shorter token sequences correspond to lower inference time. Token efficiency is commonly quantified by the compression rate , expressed as the average amount of raw text (e.g., UTF-8 bytes) encoded per token; a higher rate therefore indicates a shorter token sequence (Meister, 2026; Liu et al., 2025a). An important objective in tokenizer design is therefore to improve compression rate under a fixed vocabulary budget while preserving or improving downstream model performance. 1arXiv:2610.01127v1 [cs.CL] 1 Oct 2026 Preprint. Technical Report. Widely used tokenizer training methods face structural limitations in optimizing compres-sion under a fixed vocabulary budget. Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) constructs its vocabulary through a sequence of order-dependent merges (Sennrich et al., 2016). Forming a long token requires introducing intermediate merge products, some of which may remain underutilized while continuing to consume vocabulary capacity (Chizhov et al., 2024). Hard pretokeniza-tion boundaries further prevent tokens from spanning multiple pretokens. Recent methods such as SuperBPE (Liu et al., 2025a) relax this boundary constraint but retain sequential merge-based construction. UnigramLM avoids merge ancestry, but it requires probability estimation over a segmentation lattice and iterative likelihood-based pruning (Kudo, 2018; Land, 2026). These approaches therefore tie vocabulary construction to either sequential merge operations or iterative refinement. This raises a natural question: can we significantly improve token efficiency under a fixed vocabulary budget without relying on merges or repeated vocabulary refinement? To address this question, we propose a tokenizer training approach named Counting and Filtering (CNF ) and a text encoding algorithm called Min-Cost Encoding (MCE ). In MCE, a cost function is defined for a segment in a piece of text, and the best segmentation is determined by globally minimizing the overall segmentation cost. Our cost function does not rely on merge information (as in BPE) or token probability estimation (as in UnigramLM), making MCE applicable to a wide range of vocabularies. CNF consists of two phases. In the counting phase, a raw vocabulary is built by calculating the occurrences of valid substring candidates in the training corpus and selecting the m most frequent ones. In the filtering phase, the training corpus is first segmented by MCE based on the raw vocabulary. Then the segmentation frequency of each token is calculated and the final vocabulary is constructed by filtering out from the raw vocabulary the tokens with low segmentation frequencies. Note that CNF encodes the training corpus only once, which makes the filtering step very efficient. This non-iterative CNF procedure shifts vocabulary selection from raw occurrence frequency to realized usage under MCE, yielding a vocabulary aligned with actual token usage. We evaluate our method using intrinsic tokenizer metrics across multiple text domains and vocabulary sizes, as well as downstream task performance. Our method achieves consistently better compression than the evaluated BPE tokenizers. Particularly, the MCE encoding algorithm on our mix250k vocabulary achieves 26.7% and 32.4% higher token efficiency than the o200k base and qwen250k tokenizers respectively. Experiments scaling the vocabulary to 1M entries on English web text demonstrate sustained improvements over BPE, with a token efficiency improvement of over 60% and vocabulary utilization rising from 52.9% to 96.9%. Language models trained with our tokenizers at the 1.8B and 8B scales achieve comparable average downstream performance across 11 English benchmarks to models trained with the BPE tokenizers. Our main contributions are as follows: • We introduce CNF, a merge-free vocabulary construction method that builds a raw vocabulary by counting valid substring occurrences and selects the final vocabulary through a single filtering step based on realized token usage under MCE. • We propose MCE, a segmentation algorithm that globally minimizes an encoding cost that combines token count with penalties for partial-word boundary crossings. MCE can be applied to a wide range of vocabularies, including those built from BPE, UnigramLM, CNF, and other methods. • We demonstrate improved compression and a range of intrinsic tokenizer properties, sustained compression gains with high vocabulary utilization as vocabulary size increases, and comparable average downstream performance to the evaluated baselines. 

# 2 Related work 

Subword tokenization. BPE (Sennrich et al., 2016) and UnigramLM (Kudo, 2018) repre-sent two widely used approaches to subword tokenization. BPE builds a vocabulary by 2Preprint. Technical Report. repeatedly merging the most frequent adjacent symbol pair, and encodes new text according to the learned merge order. Its intermediate merge products occupy vocabulary entries, although some are rarely used in final segmentations. PickyBPE (Chizhov et al., 2024) and Scaffold-BPE (Lian et al., 2025) address this issue by removing such tokens based on relative frequency criteria during training. UnigramLM instead starts from an overcomplete candidate vocabulary, estimates token probabilities with EM, and iteratively prunes tokens according to their contribution to corpus likelihood, using Viterbi decoding to select the most probable segmentation. MinGram (Land, 2026) simplifies this procedure through BPE-based initialization, Hard EM on minimum-token paths, and a single final score-based pruning step. Vocabulary construction and segmentation can also be studied separately: Uzan et al. (2024) compare inference methods across multiple vocabulary construction algo-rithms, while PathPiece (Schmidt et al., 2024) combines minimum-token segmentation with iterative vocabulary pruning and supports multiple initialization strategies. Our framework constructs the vocabulary through direct substring counting and a single filtering step based on MCE token usage, without sequential merges or iterative refinement. Moreover, unlike previous methods that impose hard word-level pretokenization boundaries, our framework supports multiword tokens, allowing for further sequence compression. 

Tokenization beyond word boundaries. Many subword tokenizers impose hard pretok-enization boundaries, typically based on whitespace and punctuation, which prevent tokens from spanning multiple words (Liu et al., 2025a; Schmidt et al., 2025). Early work explored incorporating statistically selected multiword expressions into tokenization, with evalua-tions primarily focused on machine translation and text classification (Kumar & Thawani, 2022; Gee et al., 2023). More recently, SuperBPE (Liu et al., 2025a) introduces a pretok-enization curriculum that first learns subwords and then permits merges across whitespace, demonstrating improvements in both compression and downstream performance through controlled language-model pretraining experiments. BoundlessBPE (Schmidt et al., 2025) selectively merges adjacent pretokens that are each represented by a single token into su-perwords, preserving complete pretoken boundaries during these merges. Although these methods support multiword tokens, their vocabulary construction and inference still rely on ordered merge operations. Our approach instead controls partial-word boundary crossings through candidate validity rules during CNF vocabulary construction and through penalties and constraints during MCE segmentation, without relying on ordered merge operations. 

# 3 Methodology 

A tokenizer is defined mainly by three elements: a vocabulary, an encoding algorithm, and a decoding algorithm. Our approach comprises two algorithms: Counting and Filtering 

(CNF ) for vocabulary construction, and Min-Cost Encoding (MCE ) for text tokenization. Decoding reduces to a simple table lookup in the vocabulary’s (ID, token text) dictionary. CNF first constructs a raw vocabulary through substring counting in the training corpus and then refines it based on token usage under MCE. Given a vocabulary, MCE selects a segmentation by minimizing an encoding cost. 

3.1 Vocabulary Construction: Counting and Filtering (CNF) 

CNF consists of two phases. In the counting phase, we construct a raw vocabulary by select-ing the most frequent candidate tokens in the corpus. In the filtering phase, we construct the final vocabulary by removing tokens from the raw vocabulary that are infrequently used when MCE tokenizes the corpus. Thus, counting measures candidate occurrences in the original text, whereas filtering measures actual token usage during tokenization. 

3.1.1 Raw Vocabulary Construction by Counting 

Given a text corpus C, we extract valid candidate tokens from each document and aggregate their occurrence counts across the corpus. Then a raw vocabulary is built by selecting the top-m candidates with the highest counts. 3Preprint. Technical Report. A critical step in the counting phase is determining which substrings qualify as valid candidate tokens. BPE vocabulary construction typically begins with pretokenization, which splits text into chunks based on delimiters (e.g. whitespace) and other rules. Merges are restricted to individual chunks, preventing tokens from spanning chunk boundaries. CNF relaxes this boundary restriction to allow words, subwords, and multiword phrases as candidate tokens. This expands the candidate space but may also introduce undesirable cross-word substrings, such as those that begin within a word. For example, consider the following text: Example text: ”1600 B.C. was a significant year in human history.” Under CNF’s validity rules, “6”, “ sig”, “significant”, “cant”, and “ year” are valid can-didate tokens. Multiword phrases such as “ was a” and “significant year” are also valid. However, “ficant year” is excluded because it spans multiple words but begins within a word. Given a text T and a substring S, we determine the validity of S using rules that account for its boundaries and context within T.Although validity checks eliminate many substrings, a document can still yield a large number of candidate tokens, making exhaustive counting costly for corpora containing millions of documents. We address this challenge with an efficient counting algorithm for raw vocabulary construction, summarized in Algorithm 1. The algorithm uses two main strategies: 

Multi-round counting with early stopping. We construct the raw vocabulary in multiple rounds over consecutive, non-overlapping length intervals starting at length one, with shorter candidates processed first. Each interval r = [ rmin , rmax ] ∈ R has an associated frequency threshold tr. At the start of each round, we build a byte trie from all candidates retained in earlier rounds, storing their corpus-wide frequencies for comparison with the current threshold tr during enumeration. The trie is empty in the first round. The C OUNT CANDIDATES procedure enumerates substrings by starting position and in-creasing length, using two early-stopping criteria. First, if a substring is invalid and cannot become valid through further extension, enumeration stops at that starting position (e.g., in “a significant year in human history”, once the substring “ficant ” is reached, further extension cannot resolve its invalid start within a word). Invalid substrings that may be-come valid through extension are skipped without stopping enumeration. Second, if a valid substring within a previously processed length interval is absent from the trie or has a stored frequency below the current threshold tr, enumeration stops at that starting position. In either case, the prefix and its extensions cannot meet the current frequency threshold. Valid candidates within the current round’s length interval are counted. These criteria reduce unnecessary substring enumeration and the number of candidates stored in frequency dictionaries, reducing computation time and memory usage. 

Parallel counting and aggregation. Within each round, workers process disjoint subsets of documents in parallel, each maintaining a local frequency dictionary. We merge these dictionaries by summing the counts of identical candidates across workers, then retain candidates with corpus-wide frequencies of at least the current threshold tr. After all rounds, we select the top-m candidates by frequency from the combined set of retained candidates to form the raw vocabulary. In our experiments, it takes less than one hour to run the raw vocabulary construction algorithm (using 32 threads) on a corpus of one million English documents.

# 4 Experiments 

We evaluate CNF–MCE in terms of compression performance, vocabulary scaling, intrinsic tokenizer properties, segmentation under fixed vocabularies, and downstream language model performance. 

4.1 Experimental Setup Data. Our tokenizer experiments cover six text categories: English web text ( en-web ), English PDF text ( en-pdf ), mathematics ( math ), code ( code ), Chinese text ( zh ), and multilin-gual text ( lang11 ). The code and multilingual data span 14 programming languages and 11 languages, respectively. The datasets and their corresponding categories are listed in Appendix A.1. 

Tokenizer configuration. We construct our CNF vocabularies using a mixture of 6.9 mil-lion documents sampled from these data sources, comprising English (1M), mathematics (0.2M), code (1.7M), Chinese text (1M), and multilingual text (3M). For CNF, we use three counting rounds with candidate length intervals R = ([ 1, 12 ], [13, 21 ], [22, 30 ]) , correspond-ing frequency thresholds tr of 0, 80, and 80, respectively, and a raw vocabulary size of 

m = 5,000,000. We set the target final vocabulary size to k = 138,000 and k = 250,000 to construct mix138k and mix250k , respectively. We encode text with the resulting vocabularies using MCE, with the boundary penalty coefficient λ = 0.05 and the token preference cost 

ε(t) = 0.1/ (10 + s(t)) , where s(t) = 10,000/ (1 + 0.01 · id (t)) and id (t) denotes the ID of token t in the vocabulary. This cost assigns a smaller penalty to tokens with smaller IDs.

4.5 Segmentation with Fixed Vocabularies 

We compare various segmentation algorithms under fixed vocabularies to examine how the encoding algorithm affects compression and segmentation. 

Evaluation protocol. We compare BPE, MCE, and forward maximum matching (FMM) on the BPE-built qwen250k vocabulary, and MCE and FMM on the CNF-built mix250k vocabu-lary. Note that BPE tokenizaiton cannot be applied to mix250k, as no merge information is available in this vocabulary. FMM segments text from left to right by selecting the longest matching vocabulary entry at each position. We evaluate token efficiency using the number of tokens per 1,000 characters (tokens/kchar). Lower values indicate that fewer tokens are needed to encode the same text, corresponding to better compression. We further measure the similarity between two segmentations using two metrics. Distance (Dist.) is the edit distance between the token-ID sequences produced by two algorithms, divided by the length of the longer sequence. Agreement 

(Agree.) measures consistency at the byte level: each byte in the original text is assigned the ID of the token covering it, and agreement is the proportion of byte positions assigned the same token ID by both algorithms. Lower Distance and higher Agreement indicate more similar segmentations. We also report Boundary-crossing , the proportion of tokens in a segmentation that incompletely span multiple word-like units, where the units are defined in Section 3.2. A token counts as a crossing if it spans multiple units but starts inside the first unit or ends inside the last, as the token “ is a ” in “ is ancient ”. Tokens covering a sequence of complete units do not count as crossings. 

Results with the BPE vocabulary. Table 5 compares the three encoding algorithms on 

qwen250k . MCE produces the fewest tokens across all four datasets. These results show that MCE can improve compression even when using a vocabulary constructed by BPE. The table also reports pairwise segmentation comparisons. Agreement between MCE and BPE exceeds 96% on English web, mathematics, and Chinese text. Thus, without relying on BPE’s merge order during encoding, MCE produces segmentations highly similar to those of BPE while improving compression. This demonstrates its applicability to the existing BPE-built vocabulary. Agreement is lower on Python code (87.50%), indicating that the degree of similarity varies across domains. Two examples illustrate how the algorithms differ in segmentation. For text “ (taxonomy ”(not including the quotes), BPE produces “ (t ∥ax ∥onomy ” and FMM produces “ (t ∥axon ∥omy ”, 11 Preprint. Technical Report. whereas MCE yields “ (∥taxonomy ”, reducing the token count from three to two while keep-ing the word intact. For text “ memory efficient ”, all three methods produce three tokens: BPE and FMM yield “ memory ∥ eff ∥icient ”, while MCE yields “ memory ∥ ∥ efficient ”, sep-arating the underscore and preserving both words as complete tokens. More examples are provided in Table 11. 

Results with the CNF vocabulary. Table 6 compares MCE and FMM on mix250k . MCE consistently achieves greater token efficiency than FMM across all four data categories. The efficiency advantage is more pronounced when comparing MCE with mix250k against BPE with qwen250k (e.g., on English web text, token usage decreases from 222.5 to 168.0 tokens/kchar, a reduction of approximately 24.5%). Pairwise comparisons reveal noticeable differences between the segmentations produced by MCE and FMM under the same vo-cabulary. We further examine Boundary-crossing: MCE exhibits no incomplete crossings of multiple word-like units across the evaluated data categories, whereas FMM has nonzero rates in all four. These results suggest that MCE improves token efficiency while avoiding partial-word boundary crossings, thereby helping to preserve the semantic integrity of the resulting tokens. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of MCE across vocabularies. MCE keeps high token efficiency on both BPE- and CNF-built vocabularies without relying on merge rules, while avoiding incomplete crossings of word-like units. 

4.6 Downstream Task Evaluation 

Although our method has demonstrated advantages in intrinsic tokenizer properties, further evaluation on downstream tasks is needed to assess its practical effectiveness. We train language models from scratch that differ only in their tokenizers and evaluate them across a range of benchmarks. 

Evaluation protocol. We train language models from scratch at the 1.8B- and 8B-parameter scales. At 1.8B, we compare tokenizers using the BPE-built qwen152k vocabulary and our CNF-built mix138k vocabulary; at 8B, we compare qwen152k with our CNF-built mix250k 

vocabulary. Within each model scale, only the tokenizer differs; the remaining model and training settings are held fixed. Our training recipe is based on SmolLM3 (Bakouch et al., 2025). We evaluate checkpoints at the end of the phase-1 and the decay phase . Detailed model configurations are provided in Appendix B.1. We evaluate on 11 English benchmarks, described in Appendix B.2. These benchmarks cover five categories: knowledge (ARC, MMLU, OpenBookQA, and TriviaQA), math (GSM8K), coding (HumanEval), commonsense (CommonsenseQA, PIQA, and WinoGrande), and language understanding (HellaSwag and LAMBADA). All tasks use zero-shot evaluation except GSM8K, which uses five-shot evaluation. We report exact match (EM) for GSM8K, LAMBADA, and TriviaQA, pass@1 for HumanEval, and accuracy under cloze prompting (CP) for the remaining tasks. The average is the unweighted mean across the 11 benchmarks. Further evaluation details are provided in Appendix B.3.

# 5 Conclusion 

We introduced CNF–MCE, a merge-free, non-iterative tokenization framework that sepa-rates vocabulary construction from segmentation. CNF combines direct substring counting 13 Preprint. Technical Report. with a single filtering step based on realized token usage. MCE globally minimizes an encoding cost combining token count with penalties for partial-word boundary crossings while allowing multiword tokens. CNF–MCE offers several advantages over BPE, includ-ing higher token efficiency, greater scalability, and lower dependency. Experiments show consistent compression gains over the evaluated BPE tokenizers across six text categories and improvements in other intrinsic metrics. On English web text, CNF–MCE sustains compression gains and high vocabulary utilization up to 1M entries. Models trained at the 1.8B and 8B scales achieve comparable average performance to the Qwen tokenizer baseline across 11 English benchmarks. These findings support CNF-MCE as an effective approach to improving token efficiency while preserving competitive downstream performance. Future work will involve comparing downstream performance across a wider range of vocabulary sizes, model architectures, model sizes, languages, and benchmarks.

B.2 Benchmarks 

We summarize the 11 English benchmarks used in Section 4.6. They cover scientific and commonsense reasoning, mathematics, code generation, contextual word prediction, and factual and subject knowledge. The shot settings are specified in the main text; evaluation methods and metrics are detailed in Appendix B.3. 

ARC : A multiple-choice science QA dataset split into Challenge (hard) and Easy sets, testing grade-school level reasoning (Clark et al., 2018). 

CommonsenseQA : A multiple-choice dataset that requires commonsense knowledge, de-rived from ConceptNet, with questions that often require inferential reasoning beyond the surface text (Talmor et al., 2019). 

GSM8K : A dataset of grade-school math word problems, testing multi-step arithmetic reasoning and language understanding (Cobbe et al., 2021). 

HellaSwag : A commonsense NLI dataset where a model must choose the most plausible continuation for a given context, with adversarially filtered examples to avoid simple pattern matching (Zellers et al., 2019). 

HumanEval : A code generation benchmark containing 164 handwritten programming problems with unit tests, used to evaluate functional correctness of generated code (Chen et al., 2021). 

Lambada : A cloze-style word prediction task in which models must predict the final word of a passage, requiring broader contextual understanding (Paperno et al., 2016). 

MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding): A broad multitask benchmark covering 57 subjects (e.g., math, history, law) in a multiple-choice format, designed for few-shot evaluation (Hendrycks et al., 2021). 

OpenbookQA : A multiple-choice QA dataset requiring reasoning over a small set of pro-vided facts (an ”open book”), testing the ability to combine knowledge with explanation (Mihaylov et al., 2018). 

PIQA (Physical Interaction Question Answering): A multiple-choice QA dataset focused on physical commonsense reasoning, where models must choose the most sensible solution for everyday tool-use and object manipulation tasks, with questions designed to require real-world physical knowledge rather than language patterns (Bisk et al., 2020). 21 Preprint. Technical Report. 

TriviaQA : A large-scale reading comprehension dataset with question-answer pairs from trivia websites, requiring fact retrieval and reasoning across documents (Joshi et al., 2017). 

Winogrande : A scaled-up version of Winograd Schema challenge, a coreference resolution task that requires commonsense reasoning to resolve ambiguous pronouns (Sakaguchi et al., 2020). 

B.3 Evaluation Methods and Metrics Accuracy under cloze prompting (CP). For ARC, CommonsenseQA, HellaSwag, MMLU, OpenBookQA, PIQA, and WinoGrande, we use cloze prompting. Each candidate answer is concatenated with the question or context to form a complete text sequence. We compute the model’s perplexity for each sequence and select the candidate with the lowest perplexity (PPL). The reported score is accuracy, i.e., the percentage of examples for which the selected candidate matches the reference answer. Since base models have not been trained to follow instructions, multiple-choice prompting (MC) typically requires in-context examples to elicit option labels, whereas CP does not require such examples and thus supports zero-shot evaluation. 

Exact match (EM). For GSM8K, LAMBADA, and TriviaQA, we report the percentage of predictions that exactly match an accepted reference answer after task-specific answer extraction and normalization. 

Pass@1. For HumanEval, we report pass@1, which measures the probability that a single generated program passes all unit tests for a problem, averaged across problems. 22
