# Abstract 

Language models process and generate text sequentially in token units, and the tokenizer determines how much text each inference step covers. Under standard tokenization, a short English phrase such as “On the table.” is usually produced as four separate predictions for the preposition ( On ), article ( the ), noun ( table ), and punctuation ( .), where each consumes a sequence position and adds inference cost. We introduce C OBPE, a com-positional tokenization approach that represents such phrases as a lexical base token ( table ) attached with a small set of reusable surface modifiers, composed in embedding space at input and predicted jointly at output. In controlled pretraining from scratch at 780M and 1.3B scales, C OBPE short-ens sequences by 30% and improves average downstream performance by 1.2 points relative to standard BPE under matched training compute. Our results suggest that part of what is now expressed through token sequences can instead be modeled through structured representations, opening a broad design space for more token-efficient and capable language models. 1

# 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) do not operate directly on raw text, but instead consume 

token sequences, most commonly produced by Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE; Sennrich et al., 2016). To build the model’s token vocabulary, such methods first split text into whitespace-and punctuation-bounded chunks in pre-tokenization , and then allocate tokens to frequent byte sequences within chunks (OpenAI, 2024). While effective, this process results in units that inherit pre-tokenization boundaries and follow surface string structure, creating two related inefficiencies. First, very common sequences such as “ on the ” cannot become a single token (Liu et al., 2025). Instead, the model must process them using multiple sequential steps, with items that carry different amounts of content such as “ the ”, “ table ”, and “ ,” all requiring the same amount of compute. Second, the same interface treats near-duplicate forms such as table , capitalized Table ,and space-prefixed table as separate vocabulary entries. As a result, related tokens are represented using inconsistent symbols and require the model to learn their relation from data (Sch¨ afer et al., 2024; Kaplan et al., 2025), while also consuming token slots in the size-limited vocabulary that could otherwise support other languages or domains (Reif et al., 2025). These choices can materially affect model efficiency, as training and inference costs depend directly on the number of tokens used to represent text (Ahia et al., 2023; Ali et al., 2024). Overall, this suggests there is still substantial room to improve compression beyond standard sub-word tokenization. In this paper, we introduce a Co mpositional BPE tokenization approach (C OBPE). C OBPE segments text into compound tokens , each composed of a base token and a small set of surface modifiers . The base token serves as the lexical anchor, while the modifiers encode leading space, capitalization, prepositions, determiners, and surrounding punctuation (see Figure 1, top). # 2 Tokenization beyond flat sequences 

In modern LLMs, tokenization determines how much text each model step represents. Because every sequence position incurs computation, this choice directly affects training and inference costs (Ahia et al., 2023; Petrov et al., 2023). The standard approach is BPE tokenization with word-boundary pre-tokenization (Sennrich et al., 2016; Dagan et al., 2024), which greedily builds a token vocabulary of a target size before pretraining. Given a large text sample, the text is first split into word-bounded chunks in pre-tokenization, and the vocabulary is then built by repeatedly merging the most frequent adjacent character or sub-word sequences within those chunks until the target size is reached, allocating tokens to the results of each merge. At sufficiently large vocabulary sizes, BPE is highly effective for compression: in English, modern tokenizers use roughly 1.2 tokens per word on average (Ali et al., 2024), with most words represented by a single token, each receiving its own inference step. However, this design allocates similar compute to process function words —frequent words expressing grammatical relations ( the , of , on )—as it does to content words , which carry most of the lexical meaning (such as nouns, main verbs and adjectives). Punctuation is treated similarly: with pre-tokenization, punctuation marks are typically separate tokens rather than part of surrounding words. Importantly, this suggests that even after BPE’s strong compression, considerable compute is spent on processing standalone grammatical tokens. Recent work relaxes word-boundary constraints in pre-tokenization and allows multi-word tokens to form (e.g., “ in the ground. ”), substantially improving compression and downstream efficiency (Liu et al., 2025; Schmidt et al., 2025). This compression, however, is phrase-specific: for example, instead of representing “ the ” as a reusable unit across contexts, the vocabulary must allocate separate entries to combinations such as “ the ground ”, “ the earth ”, and similar phrases. Pushing this compression strategy further requires adding increasingly specific entries such as “ in the ground? ” or “ On the ground! ”, making it impractical to capture the richness of language even under large vocabulary sizes.

# 3 COBPE: compositional BPE tokenization 

The potential to compress frequent grammatical markers (§2) motivates a simple reformula-tion of standard BPE: rather than dedicating separate prediction steps or vocabulary entries to frequent grammatical material or surface variation, we can represent these as modifiers 

attached to lexical base tokens and process each composition in a single inference step. We call this approach Compositional BPE tokenization (C OBPE). We next define the to-kenizer and its modifier inventory (§3.1), show how it improves compression relative to BPE (§3.2), and describe the adaptation needed for a language model to consume and generate compound tokens (§3.3). 

3.1 Definition 

Standard BPE segments a phrase like “ in the ground, ” into four tokens: preposition, de-terminer, noun, and punctuation. Under C OBPE, such spans are represented as a single structured unit we call a compound token . Each compound token is built around a lex-ical base token , along with a small set of attached modifiers encoding frequent markers such as determiners, prepositions, and punctuation. Thus, the span above is represented as the base token ground along with the modifiers preposition=in , determiner=the , and 

punct-suffix=, .BPE also treats common surface variants as distinct token strings. For example, ground ,

Ground , and ground (with a leading space, as in mid-sentence occurrences) are separate tokens despite sharing the same lexical core. C OBPE factors out this variation by keeping the base token fixed and representing leading space and capitalization as dedicated modifiers. We focus on English and define eight modifier groups: four categorical groups for determiners, prepositions, prefix punctuation, and suffix punctuation; three binary indicators for whether the base token, determiner, and preposition are capitalized; and one binary indicator for the presence of a leading space before the span. 3.2 Compression relative to BPE 

COBPE improves compression through two complementary mechanisms. First, function-word and punctuation modifiers shorten sequences by absorbing frequent grammatical material into a single prediction. Second, modifiers for leading space and capitalization let related surface forms share a single lexical token (Reif et al., 2025), freeing vocabulary capacity from near-duplicate tokens and allowing additional unique tokens to be allocated. We measure compression with bytes per token (BPT), the average number of UTF-8 bytes covered by each token on a large corpus; higher BPT means that the same text is represented using fewer tokens. We construct BPE and C OBPE tokenizers on ClimbMix (Diao et al., 2025) and evaluate BPT on FineWeb (Penedo et al., 2024). For each tokenizer, we plot the BPT as a function of vocabulary size. To isolate the contribution of different modifier types, we also build C OBPE variants incrementally: first adding leading-space and capitalization modifiers, then determiners and prepositions, and finally punctuation. We report the results in Figure 1 (bottom). Sharing entries across capitalization and leading-space variants already improves BPT at smaller vocabulary sizes, indicating that BPE spends much of its vocabulary on near-duplicate forms. For example, in a 32k BPE vocabulary, 15% of entries correspond to capitalized variants and 12% to space-prefixed forms; under C OBPE, these are represented through modifiers, allowing new, unique tokens to be allocated at the same vocabulary budget. Adding determiners, prepositions, and punctuation leads to large, consistent improvements across all vocabulary sizes, as these markers occur frequently enough to collapse many multi-token spans into a single compound token. Overall, C OBPE improves BPT by roughly 40% across vocabulary sizes. This suggests that much of the remaining compression potential beyond standard BPE lies in modeling local structure around words, not only in better segmenting the words themselves.

Evaluation. Because C OBPE changes both the prediction unit and the factorization of text probability, its token-level likelihoods are not directly comparable to those of a standard autoregressive language model. A single C OBPE prediction may jointly determine a base token along with a determiner, preposition, capitalization, and punctuation, whereas a standard BPE model would treat these as separate (and often more easily predictable) tokens. Moreover, each C OBPE position scores every modifier group, including inactive labels for unused modifiers. Byte-normalized likelihood therefore remains well defined, but measures this full structured prediction problem rather than sequence compression or compute efficiency alone. We therefore evaluate C OBPE primarily through downstream performance, including both likelihood-based benchmarks and open-ended generation tasks, providing a natural basis for comparison across different language modeling setups.

We then analyze inference speed (§4.4) and C OBPE compression beyond English (§4.5).

Tokenization-sensitive evaluation tasks. Although C OBPE improves the 30-task average, its largest regressions occur on LAMBADA and Dyck Languages. Examining their formats suggests that both are partly driven by evaluation inputs that are atypical under C OBPE tokenization. LAMBADA asks the model to predict the final word withheld from a passage. If its prompt ends in an attachable marker, as in “Tom opened the” with target “door”, withholding the target leaves “the” as a standalone base token, although C OBPE would normally see it as a modifier. To test this boundary effect, we exclude prompts ending in an attachable determiner or preposition, retaining 2,660 of 5,000 examples. On this subset, the 24-layer C OBPE model rises from 29.7 to 35.5 EM, whereas BPE changes from 34.5 to 32.8. Dyck Languages instead consists entirely of bracket strings such as “(()())”, where punctuation itself is the content to be modeled. Because C OBPE usually composes punctuation with lexical bases, it very rarely encounters such standalone punctuation tokens during pretraining. The original SuperBPE evaluation likewise reports regressions on both tasks (Liu et al., 2025). Together, these cases highlight that some benchmarks are sensitive to tokenizer design, requiring care when comparing tokenization methods.

# 5 Related work 

COBPE connects two lines of work: tokenization for compression, and compositional lexical representations. Standard BPE remains the dominant tokenizer in LMs (Sennrich et al., 2016), with WordPiece (Schuster & Nakajima, 2012; Devlin et al., 2019) and Unigram LM (Kudo, 2018) serving as established alternatives. Recent work shows that tokenizer design affects both compression and downstream quality (Bostrom & Durrett, 2020; Ali et al., 2024; Chizhov et al., 2024), while better compression alone does not guarantee better models (Dagan et al., 2024; Schmidt et al., 2024). Our closest comparison is SuperBPE (Liu et al., 2025), which learns atomic multi-word surface tokens beyond pretokenization bound-aries. C OBPE instead represents frequent local markers as reusable modifiers, making the approaches potentially complementary: SuperBPE could learn phrases not already captured by modifier composition. Like tokenization-free methods such as MEGABYTE and BLT, COBPE aims to cover more text per global modeling step (YU et al., 2023; Pagnoni et al., 2025), but through explicit lexical composition rather than byte patches. A second connection is to structured lexical representations. Prior work has explored morphology-aware embeddings, tokenization, or pretraining objectives (Matthews et al., 2018; Blevins & Zettlemoyer, 2019; Gan et al., 2022; Cui et al., 2022; Asgari et al., 2025; Lerner & Yvon, 2025). Our input-output parameterization follows the modeling scheme introduced by Reif et al. (2025), but targets grammatical markers rather than morphology. Related NMT systems likewise separate lexical content from surface or linguistic attributes, using inline casing tags or input features (Berard et al., 2019; Etchegoyhen & Gete, 2020) and factored prediction of lemmas with POS, gender, and number (Garc´ ıa-Mart´ ınez et al., 2016). C OBPE extends this separation to the tokenization and language-modeling interface. 

# 6 Conclusion 

We introduced C OBPE, a compositional tokenization scheme that represents text as lexical base tokens with attached surface modifiers. Across two model scales, C OBPE shortens sequences by 30% and improves average downstream performance and end-to-end training efficiency. More broadly, these results show that structure conventionally spread across token positions can instead be modeled within each prediction, making token representation part of the language-modeling interface rather than merely a preprocessing choice. This opens a broad and underexplored design space between surface-form tokenization and fully learned latent interfaces. # B COBPE implementation details 

This section records additional details on the C OBPE tokenizer construction. We specify the closed modifier inventories used in the reported experiments and the deterministic, second-stage composition pass. 

B.1 Modifier inventories 

All reported C OBPE experiments use the same English modifier inventory: determiner and preposition groups, prefix- and suffix-punctuation groups, a leading-space indicator, and capitalization indicators for the base token, determiner, and preposition. The determiner and preposition groups contain ten and nine active entries, respectively. Tables 7 and 8 list the exact supported values. The modifier inventory is deliberately small and restricted to markers that can be identified and reconstructed from their surface form and local token order. No syntactic parsing is used. Listed forms are attached when they occur in an eligible local configuration, without determining their grammatical role. We therefore exclude forms such as “before” and “past”, pronouns and auxiliaries that require role disambiguation, and forms written in ALLCAPS or camelCase. When a form is excluded or cannot be attached, the tokenizer leaves it as an ordinary BPE token in the final representation. 

Modifier usage. B.2 COBPE composition pass 

At inference time, we apply a deterministic left-to-right composition pass to the first-stage BPE stream. The pass maintains a pending modifier state, absorbs eligible prefix punctuation, determiners, and prepositions before a lexical base, and attaches suffix punctuation after the lexical span is identified. Leading-space and capitalization cues are attached to the first token of a multi-token base span, while suffix punctuation is attached to its final token. Tokens that do not participate in a valid local composition remain ordinary base tokens. Algorithm 1 gives the exact pseudocode for the current implementation.

# F Multilingual tokenization analysis 

This appendix details the multilingual tokenizer analysis summarized in §4.5. We compare matched BPE and C OBPE tokenizers, describe the automatically extracted modifier inven-tories, and document the validation procedure. The full inventories reduce positions by 18.0–31.8% across the four languages. 

F.1 Data and construction 

We analyze Spanish, German, Hindi, and Indonesian on FineWeb-2 (Penedo et al., 2025). For each language, we build matched 16k-entry BPE and C OBPE tokenizers from the same training text and vocabulary budget, then measure sequence length on held-out text. All languages use the same whitespace-delimited lexical bases, spacing modifiers, and punctuation modifiers, with capitalization modifiers for the Latin-script languages. The training split is capped at 200,000 documents, with at most 10,000 Unicode characters per document. Evaluation uses 10,000 documents from the disjoint test split, capped at 10,000 characters per document. We report tokenizer compression only. 

F.2 Automatic modifier extraction 

We derive language-specific determiner and adposition inventories automatically from Universal Dependencies v2.18 annotations. The extraction retains frequent forms with consistent annotations and a consistent position relative to the lexical base, producing closed lists of surface strings that can be applied with exact matching and local attachment rules, without a language-specific parser. Forms that do not meet the filters remain ordinary BPE tokens. Hindi additionally uses a Devanagari-aware split and longest-match rule for compound postpositions, in which a multiword expression such as ke liye (“for”) functions 24 Published as a conference paper at COLM 2026 as a single postposition. Capitalization modifiers are used for Spanish, German, and Indonesian. The inventories are conservative rather than exhaustive. For Indonesian, they contain four pre-base determiners and nine pre-base adpositions. Because the shared modifier interface provides one determiner group for pre-base markers, post-base determiners and multiword or affixed components remain ordinary BPE tokens.

# G Additional related work 

Other recent work aims to make each autoregressive step carry more future information, but without changing tokenization. Hidden-state analyses show that representations at one position already contain substantial signal about subsequent tokens (Pal et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024; Samragh et al., 2025). Multi-token prediction exploits this by training models to predict multiple future tokens from a shared trunk (Gloeckle et al., 2024), while related inference-time approaches propose or decode multiple future tokens or characters and then verify them (Cai et al., 2024; Ankner et al., 2024; Ben-Artzy & Schwartz, 2025; Yan et al., 2025). These methods are complementary to ours: they modify the objective or decoding procedure, whereas C OBPE changes the representational unit. Analyses of depth also suggest that easy, high-frequency items are resolved earlier in the network, with function words and punctuation predicted earlier on average than nouns and verbs (Gupta et al., 2026), consistent with our motivation for modeling such markers compositionally. 25
