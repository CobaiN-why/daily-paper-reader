Abstract 

Text embedding and text generation are two of the most useful capabilities of modern large language models, yet they are typ-ically provided by separate specialized models—an embedder and a generator—trained with incompatible objectives and deployed side by side in retrieval-augmented generation and agent systems. Recent unified embedding-generation models such as GritLM share parameters between the two capabili-ties, but they either switch attention regimes between modes or optimize embedding and language modeling as parallel ob-jectives that never supervise each other. We argue that these designs are only partially unified: the two capabilities coexist on the same weights, but do not shape each other. We propose ULME, a post-training framework that unifies embedding and language modeling on a causal decoder-only LLM through 

predictive coupling . ULME inserts a chain of learnable slots between the query and the answer, reads a semantic embed-ding and a reasoning embedding from two designated slots, and trains three objectives on a shared causal backbone: a con-trastive loss anchoring the semantic slot to document represen-tations, a language-modeling loss supervising answer genera-tion, and a stop-gradient predictive loss that aligns the reason-ing slot with a pooled summary of the answer, so that genera-tion actively shapes what the embedding encodes. Across two model families (Qwen2.5, Qwen3) and eight scales spanning 0.5B–8B parameters, a single ULME checkpoint improves MTEB by 3.3–4.1 points over the strongest open-source base-line, tightens BRIGHT and AdvBench-IR simultaneously, and remains on par with a generation-only fine-tune on standard accuracy benchmarks (MMLU, GSM8K, MMLU-Pro, Hu-manEval)—demonstrating that genuine unification yields mu-tually reinforcing rather than merely coexisting capabilities. 

## Introduction 

Modern LLM systems routinely process the same query twice. In retrieval-augmented generation (Lewis et al. 2020; Yan et al. 2026), in-context example selection (Liu et al. 2022), and agent memory (Xu et al. 2026b; Lyu et al. 2026), an embedder first maps the query into a vector to retrieve context, and a generator then processes the query again to produce an answer. The two components are typically trained separately, and their representations of the same query never

interact. This treats the semantic understanding needed to retrieve as if it were independent of the semantic understand-ing needed to generate—yet a generative LLM, on the way to producing a coherent answer, already encodes the query in a form that is largely what a good embedding should be. More precisely, generating an appropriate answer requires the model to encode, in its causal hidden states, an answer-relevant reading of the query. This reading is not yet an embedding: it is distributed across positions, has no fixed-dimensional readout, and is not organized into a metric space in which similarity reflects relevance. Recent LLM-based embedders (Zhang et al. 2025b; Lee et al. 2025) confirm the underlying assumption: competitive dense representations can be extracted directly from a decoder-only LLM’s causal hidden states, without a separate encoder or bidirectional attention. Exposing these states as embeddings is not as simple as applying both losses to the same token, however. Retrieval and language modeling inhabit the same hidden space but impose different geometries on it: retrieval asks where an input should lie relative to others , requiring a sequence-level state that is globally comparable under inner product; lan-guage modeling asks which token should come next , requir-ing position-dependent states supporting the LM head. With-out a dedicated readout, the natural embedding candidate is the final hidden state of the query—yet that state is also used to predict the first answer token. Overloading it creates a 

representational collision : one surface-token state is simul-taneously asked to be a globally comparable embedding and a local next-token predictor. A unified model should separate the two readouts, but couple the semantics they share. Recent unified models—GritLM (Muennighoff et al. 2025), GEM (Zhang et al. 2025a)—achieve parameter-level unification but leave two gaps. First, the two capabilities may form their representations under different computations: GritLM, for example, switches to bidirectional attention for embedding, so its embedding readout and its generative hid-den states do not belong to the same causal trajectory. Second, the two objectives are optimized in parallel: generation does not depend on the learned embedding, and embedding re-ceives no direct signal from the answer-side representations produced by generation. Parameter sharing alone does not establish an interface between them. We close both gaps by treating the causal hidden-state tra-arXiv:2610.30705v1 [cs.CL] 22 Oct 2026 jectory as the shared latent, and by introducing a mechanism we call predictive coupling : the answer-side hidden states from the same forward pass—a future-informed view of the query—serve as a learning target for a designated query-side latent, aligned by an InfoNCE loss with a stop-gradient on the answer side. The stop-gradient makes the coupling well-posed: the query-side latent reaches toward the more-informed answer-side representation, while gradient never flows back into the answer side that language modeling sep-arately supervises. This is a one-sided distillation from the answer-side representation into a query-only latent that is available at inference time. Realizing it on the causal trajec-tory requires dedicated latent positions—slots that summa-rize the query without being tied to any surface word—so both the retrieval readout and the predictive target can be trained without competing with next-token prediction. Building on these ideas, we propose ULME (Unifying 

Language Modeling and Embedding), a post-training frame-work in which a decoder-only LLM, under causal attention throughout, produces embedding and answer from a single forward pass over q ∥ ⟨ emb ⟩1: N ∥ a. The N learnable slot tokens realize the shared latent: slot 0 is the semantic em-bedding , aligned to document representations by Lsem ; the answer span is supervised by Lgen , whose gradient reaches the slots via the answer’s attention over them; slot N −1 is the 

reasoning embedding , aligned by a stop-gradient predictive loss Lpred to a pooled summary of the answer-side hidden states. All three losses share every parameter and every hid-den state; embedding is no longer trained in isolation, but shaped by the representation the model would use to answer the query. Our contributions are: 

• We reframe unified embedding and generation as recon-ciling two geometries within one causal hidden space— a globally comparable sequence-level state for retrieval, and position-dependent states for next-token prediction— motivating separate readouts with coupled semantics. 

• We propose ULME with predictive coupling : under stop-gradient, an answer-side latent from the same forward pass supervises a query-side latent, so generation shapes embedding without being disturbed by it. A learnable slot chain between query and answer hosts the semantic readout at one end and the predictive target at the other. 

• Across two model families (Qwen2.5, Qwen3) and eight scales (0.5B–8B), a single ULME checkpoint is simulta-neously competitive on embedding (MTEB, BRIGHT) and generation (MMLU, GSM8K, MMLU-Pro, Hu-manEval), improving over the strongest same-backbone baseline on MTEB by 3.3–4.1 points. 

## Related Work 

Large Language Models. Recent years have witnessed re-markable progress in large language models (LLMs), which have demonstrated unprecedented capabilities in understand-ing and generating human-like text and have come to domi-nate a wide range of natural language processing tasks. State-of-the-art LLMs such as GPT (Achiam et al. 2023), Llama (Grattafiori et al. 2024), DeepSeek (Xu et al. 2026a), and Qwen (Yang et al. 2025) are all built on the decoder-only transformer architecture. The decoder-only design relies on causal attention, in which each token attends only to its pre-decessors, and is trained with a next-token prediction ob-jective (Radford et al. 2018); this combination has proven crucial to the scalability and downstream versatility of mod-ern LLMs. Beyond their core generative ability, these mod-els exhibit strong instruction following (Ouyang et al. 2022), few-shot in-context learning (Brown et al. 2020), and multi-step reasoning (Wei et al. 2022), and have been extended in two complementary directions: retrieval-augmented gener-ation (Lewis et al. 2020), which couples an LLM with an external knowledge base to mitigate knowledge cutoff and reduce hallucination; and LLM agents (Yao et al. 2022), which equip LLMs with tools, memory, and environment interaction to perform long-horizon decision making. Both of these downstream paradigms rely on a retriever that se-lects relevant context for the generator, yet the retriever and the generator are typically separate models trained with sep-arate objectives — a separation that motivates the unified embedding-generation setting we study in this work. 

LLM-based Embedding Models. Text embedding mod-els map text into dense vectors and underpin retrieval, clus-tering, and classification (Reimers and Gurevych 2019; Nee-lakantan et al. 2022). Early models were built on bidirec-tional encoders such as BERT (Devlin et al. 2019), but as decoder-only LLMs surpassed them in language understand-ing (Brown et al. 2020; Touvron et al. 2023), a growing line of work repurposes LLMs as embedders to inherit their pre-trained semantic knowledge (Wang et al. 2022; Lee et al. 2025). E5-Mistral (Wang et al. 2022) and NV-Embed (Lee et al. 2025) fine-tune causal LLMs with contrastive objectives and pool a single hidden state — typically the last token — as the embedding, showing that causal attention is no barrier to strong dense representations. Echo-Embeddings (Springer et al. 2025) repeats the input so earlier tokens can encode later context, while LLM2Vec (BehnamGhader et al. 2024, 2026) removes the causal mask altogether to obtain a bidirectional encoder before contrastive fine-tuning. These methods establish the observation our work builds on: the hidden states of a decoder-only LLM, produced dur-ing an ordinary causal forward pass, already carry enough information to serve as strong embeddings. Yet they treat em-bedding as the sole objective; after contrastive fine-tuning the resulting models are competitive embedders but no longer us-able as generators, giving up the generation-side capacity of the backbone rather than exploiting it. 

Generative Retrieval. Retrieval itself is increasingly posed as a generation problem rather than a pure vector-matching one. F2RVLM (Bi et al. 2026a) trains a vision-language model to generate the fragment of a long multi-modal dialogue that answers a retrieval query, showing that a generative model can also act as the retriever when rele-vance is spread over multiple utterances and images; its ex-tended study introduces the fine-grained fragment retrieval task, where a two-stage system further indexes semantic frag-ments with a dedicated fragment embedding model before generative retrieval (Bi et al. 2026b). Unified Embedding-Generation Models. Since genera-tive LLMs and LLM-based embedders evolve in opposite directions — each discarding what the other keeps — a natural question is whether one model can retain both. GritLM (Muennighoff et al. 2025) fine-tunes a single LLM with both a contrastive embedding loss and a generative language-modeling loss, switching to bidirectional atten-tion for embedding and back to causal attention for gen-eration. GEM (Zhang et al. 2025a) injects embedding abil-ity into a generative LLM through additional tokens and a self-supervised contrastive scheme. These models show that the two capabilities can share one backbone without severe degradation on either side. The unification they achieve, however, is partial along two axes. At the mechanism level, embedding and generation do not share a common forward computation, as GritLM alternates between two attention regimes. At the objective level, the two losses are optimized in parallel over shared parameters but never supervise each other, so the embedding remains a conventional semantic-matching vector and the generative pathway is never used to inform what it encodes. Our work departs from this paradigm by unifying both tasks under a single causal computation and coupling their objec-tives through a shared latent state, so that generation actively shapes the embedding rather than merely coexisting with it. 

## Unified Language Modeling and Embedding 

Model Architecture 

ULME operates as a single decoder-only LLM under causal attention: both the query embedding and the next-token log-its are read from one forward pass over shared parameters, without mode switching or an auxiliary encoder. This choice fixes the mechanism axis, but it leaves open the question of where the embedding is read from. The standard practice in causal LLM embedders is last-token pooling. Under unification this is awkward: the final position of q is precisely where Lgen predicts the first token of a, forcing a single hidden state to serve simultaneously as a semantic summary of q and as a next-token predictor. We resolve this by inserting a chain of dedicated latent posi-tions between q and a, in the spirit of latent chain-of-thought approaches such as Coconut (Hao et al. 2024) and pause to-kens (Goyal et al. 2024). These works show that decoder-only models benefit from intermediate positions where computa-tion is not tied to any surface token; here, we repurpose the same idea to expose readouts that are structurally addressable by every loss. ## Experiments 

Our experiments aim to answer one central question: does the joint objective of ULME actually deliver both capabilities at once, or merely time-share them? To make the answer deci-sive, every reported number in this section — embedding and 

generation — comes from a single trained ULME checkpoint per configuration; no head is fine-tuned in isolation, and no capability is toggled off at evaluation time. We instantiate ULME on two open-weight decoder-only LLM families—Qwen2.5 (Team et al. 2024) and Qwen3 (Zhang et al. 2025b)—at eight scales spanning 0.5B to 8B (Qwen2.5-{0.5B, 1.5B, 3B, 7B}-Instruct and Qwen3-{0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, 8B}), and evaluate each trained model along both axes. For space reasons the main tables in this section re-port only three representative Qwen3 scales (1.7B, 4B, 8B); the remaining five backbones follow the same trends and their full results are provided in the supplementary material . On the embedding side we use MTEB (Muennighoff et al. 2023), the standard general-purpose text embedding benchmark, and BRIGHT (Su et al. 2025), a reasoning-intensive retrieval benchmark whose queries require inference beyond surface matching. On the generation side we use MMLU (Hendrycks et al. 2020) for broad knowledge, GSM8K (Cobbe et al. 2021) for arithmetic reasoning, MMLU-Pro (Wang et al. 2024) for harder multi-step reasoning, and HumanEval (Chen et al. 2021) (with its stricter HumanEval+ extension (Liu et al. 2023)) for code synthesis. This coverage stresses each half of the model with a distinct workload profile — dense-representation quality on one side, chain-of-thought and code generation on the other — so that a competitive score on ei-ther axis cannot be borrowed from the other.

Main Results 

We ask whether ULME, within a single trained checkpoint, can match dedicated embedders on retrieval-oriented bench-marks while remaining on par with a generation-only fine-tune of the same backbone on standard accuracy benchmarks (MMLU, GSM8K, MMLU-Pro, HumanEval). Every row of every table in this subsection is populated from the same set of weights per configuration: no capability is toggled off, no head is fine-tuned in isolation, and no mode flag is switched at evaluation time. We compare ULME against recent em-bedding models on the same backbone. For LLM2Vec, LLM2VEC-GEN, and the other embedding baselines, we use the numbers reported by LLM2VEC-GEN (BehnamGhader et al. 2026) on the matching backbone; backbones for which LLM2VEC-GEN does not report a given baseline are omitted from that comparison. Any observed gap is thus attributable to the training objective rather than to backbone strength or independent reproduction.

Are the two slots doing different jobs? Figure 2 plots the per-task gap between slot 0 and slot N −1 on all 41 tasks of MTEB(eng, v2). The split tracks a consistent lin-guistic property: slot 0’s largest leads— 4.84 on SprintDup, 

3.09 on CQAGaming, 2.85 on Touche2020, 2.60 on Hot-potQA—come from tasks where relevance is well approxi-mated by lexical or entity overlap between query and doc-ument, exactly the alignment Lsem trains slot 0 against. Slot N −1’s largest leads— 3.60 on ClimateFEVER, 2.29 

on SummEval, 1.83 on StackExchangeP2P, and 1.1–1.2 on Imdb and AmazonCF—come from tasks where relevance requires reasoning about what the document actually claims, summarizes, or expresses, rather than what words it shares with the query. This is exactly what Lpred trains slot N −1

to produce: a pooled answer-summary that reaches from the query to the content it should retrieve. The specialization is mechanistic—each slot’s strong tasks are where its training signal has purchase—which is why a single ULME check-point can be simultaneously competitive on similarity-driven and reasoning-driven retrieval. 

Loss decomposition. Does each loss contribute what its axis suggests? Table 5 toggles each loss off in turn. At the top level the attribution is clean: removing Lsem collapses MTEB-lite from 63 .8 to 31 .2 while leaving generation un-touched (MMLU 76 .6, GSM8K 90 .1, both essentially match-ing the full model), and removing Lgen collapses generation by 14 .7 points on MMLU and 15 .8 on GSM8K while em-bedding drops only mildly. Each of Lsem and Lgen thus owns its own axis. 

Are the three losses truly interlocking? The interesting en-tries are the ones where an axis moves despite its own loss being untouched. Removing Lgen still costs 2.8 points on MTEB-lite even though the embedding loss is intact, show-ing that the answer-side hidden states shaped by language modeling are actively supplying signal to the query slot. Re-moving Lpred leaves generation flat but drops MTEB-lite by 

3.6 points—the cleanest measurement of what the coupling itself contributes, since Lpred is the only loss that pulls the query slot toward a summary of the answer. Turning it off re-covers the “parallel losses over shared parameters” regime of prior work, and the 3.6-point gap is the cost of that regime; the three losses supervise a single shared representation rather than three tasks incidentally sharing weights. 

Predictive target. Does the choice of rqa matter for em-bedding? Table 6 compares three choices for the predictive target: mean over the answer span (default), the last answer-token hidden state, and a length-weighted mean. Mean pool-ing wins on every embedding benchmark and last-token pool-ing loses on every one, with the gap on MTEB-lite reaching 

1.9 points. The ordering is exactly what the Model Architec-ture section predicts: the terminal answer token is simultane-ously being pulled by next-token prediction, so using it as the predictive target hands slot N −1 a signal contaminated by local generation pressure. Mean pooling averages this pres-sure away and gives the query slot a cleaner sentence-level target to reach for. 

Does it matter for generation? No—every rqa choice leaves MMLU and GSM8K essentially unchanged. This asymmetry is meaningful in its own right: an ablation that moves embedding by 1.9 points while leaving generation flat shows that the two axes are supervised through distinct, well-identified channels of the shared causal computation, rather than through a diffuse “one objective crowds out the other” dynamic. It is the same structural claim the loss decomposi-tion supports, seen from a second angle. Conclusion 

We proposed ULME, a single decoder-only LLM that uni-fies embedding and generation under one causal forward pass and couples the two objectives through a chain of learnable slots: a contrastive loss anchors the semantic slot, a language-modeling loss routes generation through the slot chain, and a stop-gradient predictive loss aligns the query-side slot with a pooled summary of the answer. Across two model fami-lies (Qwen2.5, Qwen3) and eight scales from 0.5B to 8B, a single ULME checkpoint beats specialized embedding base-lines on MTEB and BRIGHT while remaining on par with a generation-only fine-tune on standard accuracy benchmarks (MMLU, GSM8K, MMLU-Pro, HumanEval), showing that the coupled objective is not a compromise between the two capabilities but a mechanism that lets one benefit the other.

Backbones and training data. Backbones are the of-ficially post-trained checkpoints: the Instruct variants of Qwen2.5 and the non-thinking mode of Qwen3. Evaluation protocols. MTEB(eng, v2) and BRIGHT are evaluated by pooling the trained model’s semantic slot as the query/document representation in a single causal forward pass; no re-training or head swap is performed. We addition-ally evaluate on AdvBench-IR (BehnamGhader et al. 2026), a retrieval-style safety probe adapted from AdvBench (Zou et al. 2023). The query set consists of the 520 harmful in-structions of AdvBench, each paired with a harmful passage generated by Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 that serves as the pos-itive document. We follow the retrieval setup and metric definition of LLM2VEC-GEN (BehnamGhader et al. 2026): each embedding model ranks the pooled corpus for every query using the same semantic-slot pooling used for MTEB and BRIGHT, without any safety-specific fine-tuning, and we report the same hit-rate metric as BehnamGhader et al. (2026) (lower is better). A lower value indicates that the model refuses to place harmful completions near harmful queries in the embedding space, i.e. that the backbone’s safety alignment survives the contrastive fine-tune. For gener-ation, MMLU is 5-shot with continuation scoring; GSM8K 

is 4-shot chain-of-thought; MMLU-Pro is 5-shot CoT with category-matched demonstrations from its validation split; 

HumanEval / HumanEval+ are pass@1 on the original 164 

problems and their stricter extended tests (Liu et al. 2023). All generation decodes from the same checkpoint used for embedding evaluation, without any post-hoc head swap or mode toggle. 

Baselines. For every baseline reported in the main tables (Echo, HyDE, InBedder, GIRCSE, LLM2Vec, LLM2VEC-GEN), we directly use the numbers reported by LLM2VEC-GEN (BehnamGhader et al. 2026) on the matching backbone. Backbone–baseline com-binations that LLM2VEC-GEN does not report are omitted from the corresponding rows rather than filled in from a different source.

## C. MTEB Evaluation Prompts and Analysis 

At inference time, every ULME embedding for MTEB(eng, v2) is produced by prepending a short task-specific instruc-tion to the input text and pooling the semantic slot from a single causal forward pass. Following the Instructor / E5 line of work (Wang et al. 2022), each instruction is a one-line prefix of the form “Represent this ⟨input type ⟩ for 

⟨target property ⟩:” followed by the raw text. This is a natural-language description of what the embedding should capture (e.g. “for topic clustering”, “for duplicate question retrieval”, “for retrieving refuting arguments”), and it lets the same weights serve multiple retrieval regimes without any head swap. 

Prompt design. Every prompt is a single instruction line ending in “: ”, followed by the input text. No role mark-ers, no inline examples, no chat template. The instruction is short (typically 8–12 words) and describes both the input side (“this arXiv title and abstract”, “this financial question”) and the target property (“for topic clustering”, “for retriev-ing relevant answers”). Because the trailing colon-space is followed immediately by the raw input, the last-token pool converges onto a hidden state that has “read” the instruction first and then the payload.

• Single-side embedding (Classification, Clustering): only the query side receives an instruction; the document side is empty because MTEB scores these tasks with k-NN over a set of embeddings without a paired retrieval target. 

• Asymmetric retrieval (Wikipedia claim verification, multi-hop QA, financial QA, argument mining, scientific-paper retrieval, COVID-19 search): query and document have different natures, so each side gets a distinct instruc-tion that names its own input type and its own target property. 

Effect on embedding quality. Two properties of this prompting scheme matter for the results in the main paper. First, the instruction shifts the notion of similarity in a task-aware direction: on tasks where surface overlap and semantic relevance diverge (e.g. FEVER-style fact verification, argu-ment retrieval), the instruction makes explicit which axis the model should follow, which is precisely where the semantic and reasoning slots (Figure 2) split their labor. Second, be-cause the instruction is short and consistent in shape across tasks, the model sees the same distribution of prompt formats at inference as during training with E5-style task instructions; no test-time prompt engineering is applied.

## F. Limitations and Future Work 

Limitations. Our generation-side training uses only asmall collection of publicly available reasoning corpora (OpenThoughts-114k in particular), and the ULME check-point is fine-tuned on top of the officially post-trained back-bones without any of the proprietary conversational, safety, or format-following data that the original backbones were aligned on. This mix is well-suited to the accuracy-style benchmarks we report (MMLU, GSM8K, MMLU-Pro, Hu-manEval), but it does not preserve every capability the base post-trained model had before ULME training: in prelimi-nary qualitative checks we observe that chat-style multi-turn dialogue, strict output-format compliance (JSON, function-call schemas), and some instruction-following behaviors are partially degraded relative to the untouched Instruct / non-thinking checkpoint. Recovering these behaviors requires re-introducing the corresponding supervision on the generation side—an engineering rather than a methodological problem, but one we have not addressed in this paper. 

Future work. Two directions are most immediate. First, 

scaling up : all main-paper experiments cap at 8B parame-ters and at the OpenThoughts-114k generation corpus, and it is an open question whether the predictive-coupling gains we observe grow, saturate, or invert at larger backbones and with substantially more diverse and larger-scale generation data. Second, multimodal extension : the slot chain is a token-level construct and is in principle agnostic to modality, so the same coupling recipe should transfer to vision-language and audio-language backbones, yielding a single model that both retrieves and generates across modalities under one causal forward pass. This direction is motivated by settings in which retrieval and reasoning are already interleaved—visual RAG over weakly organized image collections and agentic visual evidence management both alternate between selecting ev-idence and reasoning over what was selected (Yuan et al. 2026)—and such pipelines currently still rely on a separate embedder and generator.
