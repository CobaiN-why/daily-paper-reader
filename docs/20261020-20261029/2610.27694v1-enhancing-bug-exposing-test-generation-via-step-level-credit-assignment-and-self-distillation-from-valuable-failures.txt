Abstract 

Large Language Models have shown strong code generation capabilities, yet their outputs often contain subtle bugs. Automatically re-pairing such code requires informative tests: inputs that expose faulty behavior, paired with correct expected outputs. Recent RL-based test generation methods apply the same outcome re-ward to all reasoning steps, which can discour-age useful reasoning and reinforce flawed steps. They also fail to effectively learn from trajecto-ries that find bug-triggering inputs but predict incorrect outputs, leaving their verified bug-triggering progress insufficiently exploited. To address these limitations, we propose SCALF, a process-supervised RL approach that com-bines Step-level Credit Assignment with tar-geted Learning from valuable Failed trajecto-ries. SCALF derives step-specific advantages from process rewards, allowing each reason-ing stage to receive its own learning signal. It further routes trajectories that expose bugs but predict incorrect outputs to on-policy self-distillation, using execution feedback to learn from their valuable bug-triggering progress. Across three benchmarks, SCALF outperforms strong prompting and post-training baselines by up to 2.97, 5.26, and 3.53 percentage points in joint success rate, output correctness rate, and bug-triggering rate, respectively. Our code and datasets are available at https://anonymous. 4open.science/r/SCALF-F4D1 .

Constructing such tests manually is time-consuming and difficult to scale, motivat-ing automated bug-exposing test generation with LLMs (Nan et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2025). Existing LLM-based bug-exposing test gener-ation methods primarily rely on prompting or task-specific post-training. Prompting-based meth-ods use general-purpose LLMs to generate test cases through zero- or few-shot prompts (Chu et al., 2025). Among post-training methods, UT-Gen (Prasad et al., 2025) fine-tunes test generators on synthetically perturbed data, while ATGen (Li et al., 2026) uses adversarial reinforcement learn-ing to dynamically generate buggy programs and train test generators with execution-based outcome rewards. However, outcome-based optimization over-1arXiv:2610.27694v1 [cs.SE] 20 Oct 2026 looks two key characteristics of bug-exposing test generation. First, generating bug-triggering inputs and their expected outputs involves interdependent reasoning stages (Prasad et al., 2025; Dinella et al., 2022). Even successful trajectories may contain flawed steps, yet a uniform outcome-based learn-ing signal rewards these steps alongside correct ones. Although process reward models (PRMs) en-able step-level supervision in mathematical (Zhang et al., 2025b; Zhu et al., 2025) and code reason-ing (Li et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025), they remain underexplored for bug-exposing test generation. Second, partial successes offer different opportuni-ties for further learning (Hossain and Dwyer, 2025; Molinelli et al., 2025; Hossain et al., 2023). As Figure 1 illustrates, a trajectory with a verified bug-triggering input but an incorrect output can yield a valid test by correcting the output. In contrast, a trajectory with a correct output but a non-triggering input still requires discovering a new input. How-ever, standard GRPO does not distinguish the learn-ing value of these partial outcomes, leaving the valuable progress in bug-triggering failures insuffi-ciently exploited. To address these challenges, we propose SCALF, a process-supervised RL approach that combines step-level credit assignment with targeted learning from valuable failed trajectories. We first train a task-specific PRM to evaluate intermediate reason-ing steps in bug-exposing test generation. During RL training, SCALF derives step-specific advan-tages from these process rewards, allowing each reasoning stage to receive its own learning signal. It further routes trajectories that expose bugs but pre-dict incorrect outputs to on-policy self-distillation, using execution feedback to guide output predic-tion while preserving their verified bug-triggering inputs. Across three benchmarks, SCALF-7B achieves the highest output correctness, bug-triggering, and joint success rates, exceeding the strongest base-lines by up to 5.26, 3.53, and 2.97 percentage points, respectively. In downstream program repair, its generated tests yield the highest repair success rate of 44.16%, using seven tests and up to three repair attempts per test. Our contributions are: • We propose SCALF for bug-exposing test gen-eration through step-level credit assignment and targeted learning from valuable failures. • We construct step-level supervision data and train a task-specific PRM for step-specific pol-icy optimization. • Experiments on three benchmarks show gains in output correctness, bug triggering, and joint success over prompting and post-training baselines, with further improvements in pro-gram repair. 

2 Related Work 

Bug-Exposing Test Generation. Traditional test generation methods, including search-based testing (Gruber et al., 2024), symbolic execution (Busse et al., 2022), and fuzzing (Herrera et al., 2021), pri-marily optimize structural code coverage, yet high coverage does not necessarily translate into effec-tive bug detection (Bodicoat et al., 2025; Abdullin et al., 2025). Bug-exposing test generation instead requires both an input that triggers incorrect behav-ior and its correct expected output, which makes such tests directly useful for test-feedback-driven program repair (Wu et al., 2026). 

LLM-based Test Generation. LLM-based test generation has been explored through prompting and task-specific guidance (Lemieux et al., 2023; Schäfer et al., 2024; Ryan et al., 2024; Hossain and Dwyer, 2025; Molinelli et al., 2025). Post-training methods further improve bug-exposing tests: UT-Gen (Prasad et al., 2025) fine-tunes on perturbed data, and ATGen (Li et al., 2026) applies adver-sarial RL with execution-based outcome rewards. However, ATGen assigns a single outcome reward to the entire trajectory, giving all reasoning steps the same credit. SCALF introduces process re-wards (Zhang et al., 2025b; Li et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025) so that each reasoning step receives its own learning signal, and further learns from the progress of bug-triggering failed trajectories by routing them to on-policy self-distillation (Zhao et al., 2026b). 

3 Problem Formulation 

Given a problem description p and its correspond-ing incorrect solution Cbuggy , our goal is to gener-ate a test case (x, y ), where x is the input and y is the expected output. Let Cref denote a validated reference solution; a successful test case should sat-isfy both Prediction Correctness , i.e., Cref (x) = y,and Bug Triggering , i.e., Cbuggy (x)̸ = Cref (x).We formulate the test generation as a Markov Decision Process. Following the prompt template provided in Appendix G, the generator πθ pro-2duces explicit reasoning steps, mainly including 

Bug Analysis , Test Design , and Output Prediction . At each step t, the state st is the pre-fix context, and the action at is the next structured component to generate. This step-wise formulation enables process rewards to provide step-specific credit for policy optimization. 

4 Dataset Construction 

To support the training of the PRM and the test gen-erator, we construct two types of training data from 

CodeContests+ (Wang et al., 2025), which provides a large collection of correct and incorrect solutions submitted by real users across multiple program-ming platforms. The construction follows a three-stage pipeline: Stage 1 filters problems for PRM training and test generator training, and Stages 2 and 3 derive step-level labels for PRM training. 

Stage 1: Difficulty Filtering. We first filter Code-Contests+ to retain problems of moderate diffi-culty, since overly easy or hard problems pro-vide weak learning signals. Specifically, for each 

(problem , incorrect solution ) pair, we sample eight test-generation responses with Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct (Hui et al., 2024) and discard pairs whose responses all succeed or all fail. For each retained problem, validated correct solutions serve as references, and clustered incorrect solutions serve as diverse buggy programs. This yields two disjoint problem sets: 2,000 problems for PRM data construction, and 1,843 problems with 15,695 

(problem , incorrect solution ) tasks for test genera-tor training and evaluation. 

Stage 2: MC-based Step Labeling. To enable the PRM to distinguish effective processes from inef-fective ones, we collect a balanced set of positive and negative test-generation trajectories. Specifi-cally, we prompt Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct to generate multiple trajectories for each pair of prob-lem description p and buggy program Cbuggy . We execute the generated test case from each trajec-tory and label the trajectory as positive if it both exposes the bug and predicts the correct expected output; otherwise, it is labeled as negative. For each (p, C buggy ) pair, we retain up to four posi-tive trajectories and the same number of negative trajectories to reduce label-distribution bias. We then decompose each trajectory into struc-tured reasoning steps and derive step-level labels through Monte Carlo estimation. Given an inter-mediate step with prefix state st and action at, we sample K = 8 continuations from the updated state st+1 = Cat( st, a t), yielding completed tra-jectories {ˆτi}Ki=1 . 6 Experimental Setup 

Benchmarks. We evaluate on three bench-marks: CodeContests+ (Wang et al., 2025), 

TestCase-Eval (Yang et al., 2025), and MBPP+Fix (Hard) (Prasad et al., 2025). For CodeContests+ ,we split the task pool at the problem level, yielding 437 evaluation tasks with no problem overlap with the training split. For TestCase-Eval , we retain 3,341 tasks after excluding those whose task con-texts exceed 2,048 tokens. For MBPP+Fix (Hard) ,we use the 170 evaluation tasks introduced by UT-Gen. Each task provides a problem description and an incorrect solution, requiring a bug-triggering input and its correct expected output. 

Baselines. We compare SCALF against two cate-gories of methods: • Prompting-based Methods: We evaluate mod-els from the Qwen2.5-Coder (Hui et al., 2024), Qwen3 (Qwen Team, 2025), Yi-Coder (01.AI, 2024), and SeedCoder (Zhang et al., 2025a) families, directly prompting them to generate bug-exposing tests without task-specific post-training. • Post-training Methods: We evaluate the 3B and 7B variants of UTGen (Prasad et al., 2025) and ATGen (Li et al., 2026), which use super-vised fine-tuning and adversarial reinforcement learning for test generation, respectively. 

Evaluation Metrics. For generated tests (x, y ),we report three metrics : • Prediction Correctness Rate (PCR, %): 

The percentage of generated test cases 

(x, y ) whose predicted output is correct, i.e., 

Cref (x) = y.• Bug Triggering Rate (BTR, %): The per-centage of generated inputs that expose the buggy behavior, i.e., Cbuggy (x)̸ = Cref (x).• Joint Success Rate (PCR ∩ BTR, %): The percentage of generated test cases that satisfy both prediction correctness and bug trigger-ing. 

Implementation Details. SCALF uses the 3B and 7B variants of Qwen2.5-Coder-Instruct (Hui et al., 2024), with policy training implemented in veRL (Sheng et al., 2025). The SFT and RL-based baselines use the same training split as SCALF. The PRM is initialized from Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct and trained with OpenRLHF (Hu et al., 2025). Training is performed on 8 NVIDIA H100 GPUs. 7 Evaluation Results 

7.1 Main Results 

Table 1 reports the main evaluation results on CodeContests+, TestCase-Eval, and MBPP+Fix (Hard). We compare SCALF with two categories of baselines: prompting-based models, including the Qwen2.5-Coder series (abbreviated as QWC in the table), Qwen3 series, Yi-Coder-9B-Chat (abbre-viated as Yi-Coder), and Seed-Coder-8B-Instruct (abbreviated as SeedCoder), and task-specific post-training baselines, including UTGen and ATGen. Based on the experimental results, we make the following observations: 

(1) SCALF consistently improves both pre-diction correctness and bug triggering. SCALF-7B achieves the best PCR, BTR, and PCR ∩ BTR on all three benchmarks. On CodeContests+, it reaches 87.41% PCR, 41.19% BTR, and 28.83% PCR ∩ BTR, improving over ATGen-7B by 5.26, 2.75, and 2.97 percentage points, respectively. Sim-ilar gains are observed on TestCase-Eval, where PCR ∩ BTR improves from 9.22% to 11.37%. On MBPP+Fix (Hard), SCALF-7B achieves 31.18% PCR ∩ BTR, outperforming the strongest baseline result of 28.82%. These results demonstrate that SCALF consistently improves both prediction cor-rectness and bug triggering, leading to higher PCR ∩ BTR across all three benchmarks. 

(2) SCALF provides consistent gains across backbone scales while maintaining strong pa-rameter efficiency. Compared with the same-scale ATGen baselines, SCALF improves PCR ∩ BTR at both 3B and 7B scales across all three bench-marks. For example, on CodeContests+, SCALF improves PCR ∩ BTR from 20.37% to 24.71% at 3B and from 25.86% to 28.83% at 7B. No-tably, despite using roughly 10 × fewer parame-ters, SCALF-3B surpasses Qwen3-32B in both PCR and BTR on all three benchmarks, and also achieves higher PCR ∩ BTR on CodeContests+ and TestCase-Eval. This demonstrates that the improve-ments of SCALF are consistent across model scales rather than being driven solely by a larger back-bone.

8 Conclusion 

In this paper, we propose SCALF, a process-supervised RL approach for bug-exposing test gen-eration. SCALF combines step-level GRPO for step-specific credit assignment with failure-routed 8OPSD to learn from trajectories that trigger bugs but predict incorrect outputs. Experiments on three benchmarks show consistent improvements over prompting-based and post-training baselines, while downstream program repair experiments demon-strate the practical value of the generated tests. To-gether, these results show that learning from both intermediate reasoning steps and valuable failed trajectories improves the generation of tests that expose bugs and provide correct expected outputs. 

Limitations 

Although SCALF improves bug-exposing test gen-eration, it has several limitations. First, to mitigate reward hacking on out-of-distribution trajectories, we apply process rewards only to fully successful trajectories, leaving failed trajectories without di-rect process supervision. Even within this scope, process rewards distinguish step quality beyond identical outcome rewards, enabling finer-grained credit assignment. Second, SCALF introduces ad-ditional training overhead through PRM scoring and self-teacher evaluation for routed trajectories. These computations provide step-level and token-level learning signals but increase training resource requirements compared with outcome-only GRPO.

A.4 Effect of the MC Labeling Threshold 

The relative-progress threshold ϵ controls how strictly a step is labeled as positive during MC-based step labeling. A smaller ϵ admits steps with lower relative MC scores, which may introduce noisy positive labels, while a larger ϵ imposes a 13 stricter criterion and may exclude useful intermedi-ate steps. We use PRM-guided beam search as a proxy evaluation of how the labeling threshold affects the PRM’s utility in test generation, without repeatedly training the full test generator. Specifically, we evaluate PRMs trained under different ϵ values on CodeContests+ using Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct without task-specific post-training as the generator. During search, the generator expands each retained reasoning path with four candidate continuations. The PRM scores all expanded paths, and we re-tain the three highest-scoring paths for the next search step. After the final search step, we select the highest-scoring trajectory to produce a single test case for evaluation. For the first reasoning step or a step whose preceding prefix has an MC score of zero, we use an absolute threshold of 0.5: the step is labeled positive if its current MC score is at least 0.5, and negative otherwise. The relative-progress threshold ϵ applies to all remaining steps. We report the joint success rate (PCR ∩ BTR), which measures the percentage of evaluated in-stances for which the generated test both triggers the bug and provides the correct expected output. Table 6 shows that the joint success rate increases from 11.44% to 15.10% as ϵ rises from 0.1 to 0.9, before decreasing to 13.96% at ϵ = 1 .1. Under the K = 8 MC estimator, ϵ = 0 .9 accepts steps whose MC scores are at least as high as those of their preceding prefixes, whereas ϵ = 1 .1 accepts only steps with higher MC scores. The decrease at ϵ = 1 .1 is consistent with a stricter criterion excluding useful intermediate steps that preserve the estimated success probability. When the PRM is applied to all samples, the policy can exploit this weakness by producing degenerate rea-soning formats that may receive favorable process scores without actually supporting the generated test case. Therefore, SCALF adopts the full-success gated strategy. C Training Curve Analysis 

To further understand how failure-routed OPSD affects the optimization process, we compare the training dynamics of standard GRPO and GRPO with routed OPSD. Figure 6 reports three comple-mentary statistics throughout training: the mean training score, the fraction of trajectories routed to OPSD, and the KL loss. Together, these curves characterize both the optimization progress of the policy and the behavior of the selective routing mechanism. 

Selective routing is sufficient to produce stronger policy improvement. As shown in Fig-ure 6(a), GRPO with routed OPSD achieves a higher mean score than standard GRPO after the early training stage and converges to a better fi-nal level. Figure 6(b) shows that only about 8– 12% of trajectories are routed to OPSD, indicating that selectively recovering a small subset of partial-success trajectories is sufficient to provide useful additional supervision. The routing rate further decreases in later training, suggesting that fewer trajectories require targeted recovery as the policy improves. 

Failure-routed OPSD induces larger but meaningful policy updates. Figure 6(c) shows that routed OPSD produces a consistently larger KL divergence than standard GRPO, indicating a stronger policy shift. Importantly, this larger devi-ation is accompanied by the higher mean score in Figure 6(a), suggesting that the additional move-ment reflects task-relevant improvement rather than unstable drift. Meanwhile, the KL curve gradually stabilizes, indicating that these stronger updates eventually reach a stable regime.

F.2 Training Algorithm 

Algorithm 1 summarizes the training procedure of SCALF described in Section 5. For each task, the policy samples a group of trajectories and obtains execution feedback for prediction correctness and bug triggering. PRM scores are added to the out-come reward only for trajectories satisfying both objectives. Step-level advantages are then com-puted across the entire rollout group, including trajectories subsequently routed to self-distillation. The execution feedback also determines the opti-mization route. Trajectories that trigger the bug but predict an incorrect output are optimized through OPSD: the current policy uses reference-derived feedback to form a teacher distribution and super-vises its unconditioned student distribution on the same sampled prefixes. All remaining trajectories are optimized through Step-GRPO, with each step’s advantage applied to its corresponding token span. 17 The losses from both routes are aggregated to up-date the policy.
