# Pairing Matters: Performance, Failures, and Efficiency in Lightweight Coding Agents 

Lu Zhao Christopher M. Poskitt David Lo 

School of Computing and Information Systems , Singapore Management University , Singapore lu.zhao.2023@engd.smu.edu.sg, {cposkitt, davidlo}@smu.edu.sg 

Abstract —Self-hosting coding agents is often necessary for software-engineering teams that must keep proprietary code and development traces within controlled infrastructure. Lightweight, open-weight models make such deployment more feasible by re-ducing hardware requirements, while strong results on repository-level benchmarks demonstrate their potential for demanding coding tasks. Deploying these models as coding agents requires pairing them with an agent harness, making their joint perfor-mance a key deployment consideration. Yet existing leaderboards primarily report fixed harness–model configurations rather than controlled comparisons across alternative pairings. To address this gap, we investigate harness–model interactions through a controlled full-factorial evaluation of six open-source harnesses and three lightweight coding models across 100 SWE-bench Verified tasks, producing 1,800 graded runs under common serving, metering, and grading conditions. For the same model, resolution rates differ by up to 44 percentage points across harnesses, while harness rankings change across models. We apply an evidence-based failure taxonomy to 1,100 unsuccessful runs and find substantial differences in failure patterns across harness–model pairings. A model’s dominant failure modes under one harness need not dominate under another. Efficiency is similarly dependent on the pairing: even among model-directed loop harnesses, charged tokens per resolved task vary by more than 20-fold. These findings make harness–model selection a central deployment decision for lightweight coding agents. Strong performance with one harness or model does not guarantee similar results with another. We translate these findings into empirically grounded practitioner guidance for pairing selection, token-efficiency assessment, and trajectory-informed harness engineering, and release the complete experimental records and analysis artifacts. 

Index Terms —coding-agent performance, harness–model pair-ing, token efficiency, trajectory-based failure analysis 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Confidentiality requirements and the need to retain con-trol over proprietary code motivate local or self-hosted deployment of coding agents [1]–[3]. Lightweight, open-weight models lower the hardware barrier to such deploy-ment, bringing it within reach of more modest comput-ing resources [3]. Their appeal extends beyond deploya-bility: recent coding-specialized models demonstrate sub-stantial capability in repository-level issue resolution. The 24B-parameter Devstral Small reports 53.6% resolution on SWE-bench Verified with OpenHands [4], while Qwen3-Coder-Next reports 70.6–71.3% across three harnesses, re-maining competitive with substantially larger models despite activating only 3B of its 80B parameters [5]. These re-sults position lightweight models as credible foundations for self-hosted coding agents, combining deployment flexibility with the capability to tackle demanding software-engineering tasks. Deploying these models as coding agents requires selecting an accompanying harness, which manages repository context, tool access, execution, validation, and patch submission [6]–[9]. The deployment decision therefore concerns a harness–model pairing, not either component alone. Yet existing evaluations offer limited guidance for this choice. Our audit covered 324 submission records across six tracks of the official August 2026 SWE-bench archive [10]. At least 223 reported results using proprietary models, compared with five reporting results for off-the-shelf models with fewer than 100B total parameters paired with established agent harnesses. These five submissions report fixed harness–model configurations, providing little sys-tematic evidence of how changing the harness affects the same lightweight model’s performance. Beyond leaderboards, model reports and harness studies typically evaluate only one or a few fixed harness–model configurations [4]–[9]. Although coding-agent performance is increasingly understood as a property of the joint system, the effects of different pairings remain insufficiently characterized. Existing evidence provides limited insight into how pairing choices affect resolution performance, failure mechanisms, and cost, or how these differences can guide pairing selection and adaptation for specific tasks and resource budgets. We address this gap through a controlled full-factorial study of three lightweight coding models and six open-source har-nesses. We pair Qwen3-Coder-Next [11], GLM-4.7-Flash [12], and Devstral-Small [4] with OpenCode [6], OpenHands [7], mini-SWE-agent [8], SWE-agent [9], AutoCodeRover [13], and Agentless [14]. Each of the 18 pairings attempts the same 100 SWE-bench Verified tasks, yielding 1,800 graded runs under common serving, metering, and grading conditions. This design examines how pairing choice shapes resolution accuracy, resolved-task overlap, failure mechanisms, model-call demand, and charged tokens per resolved task. Figure 1 summarizes the study, and Section II details the selection criteria and experimental procedures. Our results yield three principal insights for deployment. First, harness selection changes the resolution rate of the same coding model by up to 44 percentage points, and harness rankings do not transfer consistently across models. These findings emerge from three complementary analyses, organized around the following research questions:  

> •

RQ1: Pairing Performance. 

How does task-resolution accuracy vary across harness– model pairings, and how do resolved-task sets overlap across models and harnesses?  

> •

RQ2: Failure Mechanisms. 

Which failure mechanisms occur in unsuccessful runs, and how do failure profiles vary across models, harnesses, and pairings?  

> •

RQ3: Pairing Efficiency. 

How do harness–model pairings differ in model calls per run and charged tokens per resolved task? By addressing these questions, we provide an empirical foundation for understanding harness–model pairing behaviour and informing the deployment of lightweight coding agents. Our contributions are fourfold:  

> •

A systematic evaluation of lightweight coding-agent pairings. We conduct a full-factorial evaluation of six open-source harnesses and three lightweight coding-specialized models under common self-hosted serving conditions, characterizing variation in resolution perfor-mance, resolved-task coverage, and token efficiency.  

> •

An evidence-based analysis of pairing-specific failures. 

We develop a taxonomy of five behavioural failure mecha-nisms and apply it to all 1,100 unsuccessful runs, enabling comparisons of failure profiles across models, harnesses, and individual pairings.  

> •

Empirically grounded guidance for pairing-aware deployment. We recommend testing alternative harnesses before ruling out a model, and alternative models before ruling out a harness, using both resolution rate and the tasks resolved on the intended workload to guide selection. We also recommend budgeting against resolved tasks, assessing the token efficiency of lightweight models empirically, and using pairing-specific failure evidence to prioritize improvements to progress control, fault localization, and validation.  

> •

An auditable experimental record. We publicly release records for all 1,800 graded runs, including benchmark grades, available trajectories, candidate patches, and serv-ing telemetry, together with failure labels, configurations, and analysis scripts. II. S TUDY DESIGN 

We use a full-factorial design in which each selected coding model is evaluated with every harness. The following subsections describe harness and model selection, benchmark tasks, execution controls, and evaluation procedures. 

A. Agent Harness Selection 

We identified candidate harnesses through SWE-bench sub-missions, published harness papers, and GitHub searches. Eligibility required a public, version-pinnable implementation supporting headless execution and model substitution through our locally served OpenAI-compatible endpoint. Where needed, lightweight adapters handled harness invocation and connection to our locally served models while preserving each harness’s existing interaction workflow. Among the harnesses meeting these criteria, we selected six to balance research or practitioner adoption with diversity in execution control. At our July 2026 selection cutoff, their repositories had approximately 2,000 to 200,000 GitHub stars; we use these dated counts as indicators of adoption, not quality. Model-directed interaction loops. OpenCode [6], Open-Hands [7], mini-SWE-agent [8], and SWE-agent [9] follow an action–observation loop: the model selects actions using task instructions and harness-provided context, while the harness executes them and returns observations. Phased retrieval and repair. AutoCodeRover uses structure-aware retrieval followed by patch generation [13]. Fixed repair pipeline. Our evaluated Agentless [14] config-uration follows predefined localization stages and generates a single candidate patch. The model supplies stage outputs, while the harness controls progression and termination without a model-directed command interface. Internal test-based patch validation is omitted; the resulting patch is assessed by the common SWE-bench grader. 

B. Model Selection 

We considered coding-specialized open-weight models re-leased during the year preceding our July 2026 selection. Eligibility required 10–100B total parameters and public check-points that could be served on a single H100-class GPU under the evaluated serving configurations. Among models meeting these criteria, we selected three based on reported SWE-bench Verified performance, practitioner adoption indicated by Hugging Face download counts recorded at selection, and diversity in architecture, training emphasis, reasoning mode, and tool-use protocol. The selection prioritizes contrasting designs relevant to self-hosted deployment rather than aiming to represent all eligible models. 

Qwen3-Coder-Next (Qwen3-Coder). This sparse mixture-of-experts model has 80B total and approximately 3B active parameters. Its training emphasizes long-horizon coding-agent tasks, tool use, and recovery from execution failures [11]. 

Devstral-Small (Devstral-S). This dense 24B model was developed by Mistral AI and All Hands AI for agentic software engineering [4]. Its development with the OpenHands main-tainers motivates comparing its performance under OpenHands with that under other harnesses. 

GLM-4.7-Flash (GLM-4.7-F). This mixture-of-experts model has 30B total and approximately 3B active parameters and supports explicit reasoning [12]. We evaluate it with thinking enabled throughout the study, in contrast to the non-thinking configurations of the other two models. Together, the three models span dense and sparse architec-tures and differ in agentic training, reasoning modes, and tool-use protocols. These contrasts allow us to examine harness– model pairing behaviour across different model designs, while keeping conclusions scoped to the evaluated models and configurations.

D. Experimental Runtime 

We developed a common experimental runtime that prepares task repositories, invokes harnesses, serves models, and collects outputs across all 18 pairings. Harnesses run headlessly, with Docker-based environments used for task execution. Available workspaces and run outputs are retained regardless of termi-nation status. Section II-E describes the recorded artifacts, efficiency metrics, and grading procedure. 

Execution controls. All pairings shared the task set, serving infrastructure, grading procedure, and a cumulative threshold of 262,144 completion tokens per run. Harness-specific limits and timeouts provided additional execution bounds. Starting from native configurations, we adjusted selected limits in response to prolonged runs during evaluation to keep execution computationally tractable. These additional bounds are not directly equivalent across harnesses; our results therefore characterize the evaluated deployment configurations. The released artifact documents the exact settings. 

Model serving and hardware. We serve models sequen-tially through vLLM, keeping each model’s serving config-uration fixed across harnesses. All harnesses use an OpenAI-compatible interface, with model requests and token usage recorded through the common metering infrastructure. Prefix caching is enabled, with model-specific parsers configured for tool-calling requests. Inference runs on a single NVIDIA H100 NVL GPU, while harnesses execute on a separate CPU host connected through an SSH tunnel. 

E. Grading and Metrics 

We use a harness-independent measurement protocol to compare resolution performance, trajectory length, and token expenditure across pairings. The common proxy records model exchanges and token usage reported by the serving engine. Execution logs, run metadata, candidate patches, and bench-mark grades provide complementary evidence for outcome assessment and failure analysis. 

Run and Trajectory. A run is one attempt by a harness–model pairing on one task. Its trajectory is the sequence of model requests and responses, including tool calls and observations represented in those exchanges. For each run, we associate the available trajectory with its execution logs, run metadata, candidate patch, and benchmark grade. The run metadata records termination status, wall-clock duration, and patch availability. Candidate patches are retained for benchmark grading and failure analysis. The proxy uses a common recording format across harnesses. Its metering records include prompt and completion token counts and, where available, timestamps, latency, time to first token, cached-token counts, and finish reasons. 

Trajectory Length in Iterations. A run’s trajectory length is the total number of model calls made during that run. Each model request and its corresponding response constitute one iteration. When harness-initiated retries or sub-agent calls generate separate model calls, each such call is counted as a separate, additional iteration. 

Token Cost. We calculate a run’s charged-token cost by summing the prompt and completion tokens reported by the serving engine across all model calls. For execution control, we set a shared generation budget of 256K (262,144) completion tokens per run, accumulated across model calls and excluding prompt tokens. This study-specific threshold accommodates the completion-token usage observed in most runs while providing a common safeguard against excessive generation during prolonged or repetitive execution. 

Grading and Resolution. Before grading, we filter candidate patches to remove edits to tests, selected configuration files, and auxiliary files such as documentation and temporary scripts. We use the resolution outcomes reported by the official SWE-bench Docker grader [16]. Runs with missing or empty patches are counted as unsuccessful. We explicitly distin-guish exchange breakdowns, where an unrecovered interaction or environment failure prevents repair completion or valid submission, from failures involving execution progress, fault localization, validation, or repair correctness. The categories therefore capture both how the model and harness interact during execution and how the repair attempt progresses and ultimately ends. Table IV defines five mutually exclusive behavioural failure types. Criteria are applied from top to bottom, and each run receives the first matching label. We applied this taxonomy to all 1,100 unsuccessful runs through a uniform evidence-based review procedure. Au-tomated scripts extracted diagnostic indicators from proxy records, execution trajectories, and submitted patches. For each run, these indicators were assembled into an evidence pack containing the task description, condensed trajectory, final exchange, and patch text. The indicators supported interpretation of the recorded behaviour rather than determining the label automatically. Two model reviewers, Claude Sonnet 5 and Claude Haiku 4.5, assessed each run through blinded independent review using the same rubric and the ordered criteria in Table IV. During the initial assessment, each reviewer was blinded to existing labels and the other reviewer’s ratings. Cases with insufficient evidence underwent evidence enrichment and reassessment before agreement was calculated. After evidence enrichment and completion of all ratings, the reviewers agreed on 784 of 1,100 runs (Cohen’s κ = 0 .600 ). We used unweighted κ to account for agreement expected from each reviewer’s category frequencies [22]. The 316 disagree-ments underwent a separate adjudication step using Claude Sonnet 5 in fresh contexts. The adjudicator examined both ratings, their rationales, and the recorded evidence, applying the same ordered criteria rather than automatically accepting either reviewer’s label. This step produced a final label for all 316 disputed runs. As an additional quality-control step, Claude Sonnet 5 re-examined 48 cases with matching reviewer ratings in fresh contexts, checking the agreed labels against the recorded evidence and rubric. Of these, 47 labels were retained and one was reassigned to a different failure category. The released artifact documents the reviewer identifiers, prompts, individual ratings, adjudication decisions, and the spot-check correction.

Under SWE-agent, the loop/budget share is 95% with Qwen3-Coder, 35% with GLM-4.7-F, and 25% with Devstral-S. AutoCodeRover shows corresponding shares of 64%, 43%, and 11%. Thus, the prominence of loop/budget failures in these harnesses’ aggregate profiles depends strongly on which model they are paired with. This pairing dependence extends beyond loop and budget failures. For Devstral-S, insufficient validation accounts for 41% of Agentless failures, 24% under OpenHands, and 12% under mini-SWE-agent. Yet mini-SWE-agent has a much higher loop/budget share than OpenHands, at 50% versus 7%, and leaves more tasks unresolved, at 78 versus 46. Its lower validation-failure share therefore accompanies a different failure profile, not better overall performance. These results highlight the need to assess the complete pairing’s failure profile rather than individual categories in isolation. Together, these comparisons show that neither a model’s nor a harness’s aggregate profile fully characterizes the failures of its individual pairings. The specific combination must be examined to determine which mechanisms are most prominent. 

Finding 4. Harness–model pairings exhibit distinct failure profiles that are not fully characterized by either component alone. Loop or budget failures range from 3% to 95% across Qwen3-Coder pairings and from 25% to 95% across models paired with SWE-agent; insufficient validation ranges from 4% to 41% across Devstral-S pairings. A. Implications for Coding-Agent Deployment 

Drawing on the observed differences across harness–model pairings, we offer empirically grounded guidance for teams selecting and operating self-hosted coding agents and for developers improving these systems. 

For application teams selecting a lightweight coding-agent stack: compare alternative harness–model pairings to guide component selection. A model that performs poorly under one harness may perform substantially better under another; conversely, a leading harness does not retain its advantage across all models. Our observed same-model resolution gap of up to 44 percentage points shows how strongly harness choice can affect the assessment of a model. Harnesses can attenuate or amplify model weaknesses, while even strong pairings resolve only partially overlapping task sets. Teams should therefore keep harness alternatives open when selecting a model, and model alternatives open when selecting a harness. Final selection should consider both resolution performance and the tasks resolved on the intended workload. Neither a weak result nor a leading score should be treated as an intrinsic property of either component: the deployment advantage belongs to the evaluated combination of model, harness, and tasks. 

For operations teams managing execution budgets: lightweight does not necessarily mean token-efficient. Asmaller model’s deployment footprint does not determine the inference expenditure needed to resolve a task. Among iterative harnesses, charged tokens per resolved task range from 1.1 to 87.8 million—nearly an 80-fold difference. Repeated attempts, growing context, and unsuccessful runs can consume sub-stantial resources without producing additional resolved tasks. Indeed, 13 of the 18 evaluated pairings consume more tokens per resolved task than both frontier references in Figure 4. Although estimated frontier usage and differing configurations preclude conclusions about monetary or computational cost, this comparison challenges the assumption that smaller models necessarily require fewer tokens to accomplish the same work. Operations teams should budget against resolved tasks, account explicitly for expenditure on failed runs, and test whether larger execution budgets actually yield additional resolutions. More inference should be treated as an investment to justify, not evidence of productive progress. 

For coding-agent developers improving harnesses: use pairing-specific failure evidence to prioritize engineering work. Our failure analysis shows that a mechanism promi-nent in a model’s aggregate profile can vary substantially across harnesses. Aggregate resolution rates identify weaker configurations, but pairing-specific trajectories and failure profiles help identify which parts of the workflow warrant investigation. No-progress loops or budget exhaustion suggest examining progress-monitoring and recovery policies. Fault mislocalization points to retrieval and localization support, while insufficient validation motivates checks that exercise the required behaviour. These findings identify potential engineer-ing priorities, not verified remedies. Developers should use the recorded evidence to investigate both model behaviour and harness–model interaction, and evaluate whether targeted changes improve the intended pairing. 

B. Threats to Validity 1) Internal and Construct Validity: We retain a single final attempt per pairing–task combination, so run-to-run variance cannot be estimated. Although each model’s sampling settings are fixed across harnesses, serving and execution variability may still affect outcomes. For each model, coverage unions combine six attempts per task, one under each harness. They therefore neither distinguish harness complementarity from run-to-run variability nor demonstrate an advantage over six attempts with a single harness. Accordingly, we emphasize broad patterns rather than small differences. Harness-specific execution limits and timeouts may also influence resolution, failure profiles, and token expenditure, limiting attribution to harness design alone. Outcome measurement and failure labelling introduce further limitations. SWE-bench provides reproducible grading but does not assess maintainability or broader patch quality, nor establish correctness beyond the tested behaviour. Failure la-belling also involves interpretation, particularly for insufficient validation and wrong or incomplete fixes. Independent model review does not eliminate this uncertainty, which may affect both individual labels and aggregate failure profiles. 

2) External Validity: Our evaluation covers six open-source harnesses, three self-hosted coding models, and a fixed, strat-ified 100-task subset of SWE-bench Verified. The reported resolution rates describe this subset rather than estimate full-benchmark performance. Rankings and effect sizes may differ across systems or versions, non-Python projects, task distribu-tions, and activities beyond repository-level issue resolution. Generalisability also depends on serving conditions. We served all models through vLLM on a single NVIDIA H100 NVL GPU. Different sampling settings, quantization schemes, and context limits may alter resolution performance and failure profiles, while changes in caching, serving engines, or hard-ware may affect efficiency and outcomes for runs constrained by timeouts. We also evaluated only one completion-token threshold; different thresholds may change the observed results. VII. R ELATED WORK 

Coding-agent evaluation and trajectory analysis. Evalua-tions using SWE-bench [16] and SWE-bench Verified [17], including official submissions [10], model reports [4], and harness studies [9], characterize the performance of selected harness–model configurations. These results establish what the evaluated systems achieve, but do not by themselves reveal how performance changes when either component is replaced. Our full-factorial evaluation makes these comparisons explicit: each model is evaluated across six harnesses, and each harness across three models under common conditions. The resulting differences in accuracy and resolved-task coverage show which component-level strengths persist and which depend on the pairing. Trajectory studies provide complementary explanations through thought–action–result patterns [20] and repair-process analysis [15]. They identify recurring behaviours and failure mechanisms within the studied configurations. Building on these analytical perspectives, we compare failure profiles across the pairing matrix rather than characterize each system in isolation. Our results show that recognizable model-level fail-ure tendencies can coexist with substantial harness-dependent variation. This extends behavioural analysis from identifying how agents fail to examining how those failures change with the model–harness combination. 

Harness adaptation and agent evolution. Automated adap-tation studies demonstrate that harness changes can improve performance without replacing the underlying model. Yang et al. [23] show this potential for smaller models on busi-ness tasks, while Meta-Harness [24] and Agentic Harness Engineering [25] demonstrate improvements through feedback-guided harness optimization. These findings align with our observation that harness choice materially affects realized model capability. Our contribution is complementary: the controlled pairing matrix reveals where existing harnesses differ in task coverage, failure profiles, and token efficiency. These comparisons provide evidence for prioritizing model-specific harness improvements and inform model–harness co-evolution toward more effective and efficient coding agents. VIII. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

Our evaluation of 18 harness–model pairings shows that coding-agent performance depends on the pairing rather than either component alone. For the same model, harness choice changes resolution accuracy by up to 44 percentage points; no harness performs best across all models, and even strong pairings differ in which tasks they resolve. Failure mechanisms are similarly pairing-specific: a model’s dominant failure tendency can be amplified, attenuated, or redirected by the harness. Efficiency also varies substantially: among iterative configurations using the same model, harness choice changes model calls by 2.6–3.4 × and charged tokens per resolved task by 9–18 ×. These findings caution against treating a weak or leading result as an intrinsic property of a model or harness, or assuming that lightweight models are token-efficient. Practitioners should keep both model and harness alternatives open when selecting for the intended workload, account for expenditure on unsuccessful runs when budgeting for resolved tasks, and use pairing-specific failure evidence to prioritize harness improvements. Building on this system-level perspective, future work should develop methods for diagnosing harness–model interac-tions and improving their joint performance. Resolution rates and trajectory analyses show how a coding agent performs, but offer limited guidance on which interactions between model capabilities and harness policies drive success, failure, and token expenditure. A systematic framework could profile both components and use controlled interventions to identify the underlying mechanisms, guiding pairing selection and adaptation for specific tasks and resource budgets. In the longer term, these diagnoses could support a co-evolution loop: adapt the harness or train the model, evaluate the revised pairing, and use the resulting evidence to guide the next change.
